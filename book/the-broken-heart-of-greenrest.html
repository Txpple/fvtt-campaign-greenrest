PrologueWhat Nobody Knew

Long before there was a village, before there was a road, before anyone had thought to call the fog country a forest and draw it on a map, a fey lived in a valley in the Misty Forest. She was an old, deep thing of spring and growing — the kind of power that makes orchards heavy and winters mild without ever meaning to. She has forgotten her own name. Over the thousand years this story spans, she forgot almost everything. The only name left anywhere in her dreaming is Corin.

Corin was a mortal. He came walking out of the fog one morning, raven-haired and laughing at the impossible green of her valley, and she rose out of her orchard to look at him. What followed was the happiest memory anyone ever kept: seasons of him, the valley blooming a little harder every time he smiled, because she willed it so. Picnics. Harvest dances. Silver threading into the black of his hair, one visit at a time, and none in hers.

When she saw the silver she understood, in the way a thing of endless returning spring can understand anything, that he would end. So she offered him the only gift she had. Stay. Stay, and I'll make it summer for you forever. Be as I am. Never end. She said it on the best evening of all of it, with his face in her hands, and he smiled and deflected, and she laughed at that, delighted, and missed it completely. She said it again at dusk with the flowers closing, and this time it was an argument, and she was losing it. He had lived among the fey long enough to see what they become — the slow hollowing, the centuries of drifting, the beautiful creatures that forget their own names. If you take the ending out of me, there's nothing left that you love. A life isn't for keeping. It's for spending. Let me go and spend mine.

He walked down the hill toward the fog. She followed him exactly as far as she existed — to the line where her green met the mist — and stopped, because a dryad ends at her valley. The fog took him a step at a time. She never learned what became of him. Old, or happy, or nothing: his story ended somewhere past the fog, and no ending ever came back to her.

So she waited, because she could not do otherwise. The green comes back; the swallows come back; everything comes back, always. Every spring she made more glorious than the last, not from habit but as a signal-fire, a valley visible from far away so it could be found again. Decades of springs. And then there came a spring when even she knew that a mortal's handful of years was long spent — that however his story had gone, it had gone, and she would never know which ending it wore. She broke on the not-knowing.

Fey do not die. Death is a door shut to them; she could not even follow him through that. So she did the only thing left to a creature that cannot die and cannot bear to live: she tore out her own heart and shattered it. It broke into four true pieces, and the burst of it was the single most violent thing this gentle power ever did. The pieces could not die either — a fey heart cannot, and rot is death's business — so, denied the one ending flesh is allowed, they set: grief gone hard, turning in the air to warm, translucent crystal. What came to earth in four far-apart places across the fog country was not gore. It was gemstone with a heart still inside it. The same detonation tore the mists off her valley in a ring, the valley flinging back the fog that took him, one last time, too late — and the clearing it left in the Misty Forest has never closed since. Then, where she stood, she took root. She folded herself into her own tree and went down into sleep, dreaming rather than living, because living had Corin's leaving in it.

Here is the strangeness of it. Her grief had nowhere to go but her magic, and her magic knows only one verb: flourish. Her tears did not poison the valley. They bloomed it. A power of spring, weeping, is a land that cannot stop being fertile, lucky, green. Every soft winter and heavy harvest in the valley for the next thousand years was a tear that fell long ago and was still landing. She was keeping her promise — summer for you forever — to the only thing that never left.

Settlers working south down the Trade Way found it eventually: a wide green valley with no mist in it at all, the best land anyone had ever seen, standing empty. They did not know why the mists parted there and they did not ask. They called the place Greenrest — a green place to rest on a long road — and only the oldest families ever remembered the quieter second reading. They flourished beyond all sense. And they dreamed. Anyone who slept in that valley dreamed of grief, a weeping they could not place, and over the generations the town tied the dreams to the one wrong thing in its paradise: the pale, half-dead tree in the square. They guessed something old was sleeping under it, probably a dryad, and then, being practical people with a good life, they decided to live with it. The apothecary's craft produced a weekly draught that stopped the dreaming. The whole town took it up as plain custom. Within a generation or two the dreams themselves were forgotten, and as far as Greenrest was concerned there was a dead tree, there was a tonic, and life was good.

They prayed to Chauntea, the Earthmother, for the harvest that never failed, and raised her a basilica no farming hamlet has any right to. But the goddess they praised was not the power that fed them, and Chauntea knew it. She knew her name was fixed to a false idol, and that the valley's whole sweet life was drawn from a soul denied its death — the one unnatural thing the Earthmother, above all gods, cannot abide. So she refused. In all Greenrest's history she never called a cleric, never granted a spell, never sent a single miracle. And because she is the gentlest of gods, her refusal looked like nothing at all. It was indistinguishable from the good weather they credited her for.

Nobody in Greenrest knew about the shards. Nobody knew the name Corin. Nobody knew that the treasure-story the passing adventurers chased — the Broken Heart, the last company called it — had anything to do with their tree. There was nothing nefarious in Greenrest, no conspiracy, no kept secret, no cage deliberately tended. It was exactly what it looked like: a lucky, kind, comfortable village that had long ago stopped asking why.

Four hunters came down the Trade Way in the summer of the thousandth year, following a map assembled from a gnome's research toward a relic none of them understood. This is what they did.

Dramatis PersonaeThe Company

Four hunters out of Daggerford. Their own griefs were the campaign's fuel — the first night in the valley mirrored each one back at its owner, and the Heart's final arithmetic was built on two of them.

Thomas A. Invictus

Played by Robert

Human paladin of Lathander, sworn to the Oath of Vengeance. Twenty-eight, Daggerford born, a scribe in the ledger offices until six months before this story. His wife Ann and their four children were murdered by a man he had been investigating — a clerk selling names and body parts to buyers in the south — and following that trail is what surfaced the legend of the Broken Heart. He carries Oghma's symbol beside Lathander's, knows sign language, and has organised his whole life around finding one name.

Morgash the Gravemaker

Played by Anthony

Bone-white orc, fighter, a Battle Master by trade and a gravedigger before that. Lower-City Baldur's Gate; then the Flaming Fist; then the Basilisk Gate, where his company held against the netherbrain and forty became nine, and he dug the graves himself. A caravan guard for hire now, one big score from retirement. The heartbreak he keeps private: a githyanki warrior named Lae'zel, a romance that began over a whetstone and ended with I cannot love someone in this world. Goodbye, my Morgash.

Gren Greenmantle

Played by Tom

Forest gnome sorcerer, white-bearded, small, suspicious by temperament. Raised in the Misty Forest and sent away to a monastery when the silver-white fire in him surfaced — cast out, in his own words, unwanted and unloved. The village elders pressed an acorn from their heart-oak into his hand the day he left, to be planted when he finds the place he belongs. He found the shard references in an old temple ledger; the party's map is his work. Holds a standing vendetta against will-o'-wisps.

Jetten Elisedil

Played by Drew

Wood elf ranger out of the Chondalwood, one hundred and ten years old, Thomas's childhood friend from the ranch days. His sister died long ago and outliving her never healed; he wears her moonstone on a cord at his throat. A devotee of Sehanine Moonbow, lady of moonlight and dreams, which means he trusts what he sees when he sleeps. On the road to support Thomas, and for no other reason he will admit to.

Greenrest

Mayor Oswin Applewhite — warm, fatherly, mayor some fifteen years, never left the valley, never faced an election. Boasts, accurately, that there is no crying in Greenrest.

Selma — the apothecary. Brews the weekly tonic and the potions that are the town's only healing. Does not like change.

Ambrose Featherstone — keeper of the Long Rest, the inn. Gives the party the good rooms and frets over their wounds.

Mother Wend — the elderly dwarf who keeps the Full Larder. Knew all four of their names before they walked in.

Constable Warin Holt — captain of the Hedgewatch, fifty strong. We don't leave the town, and they don't come in here.

Coll — the gravedigger. The one man in town who wonders out loud. Sometimes skips his tonic.

Acolyte Enid — the church's devout young keeper. Has never once received a sign from her goddess, and never thought to ask why.

Pip — a halfling child, too young for customs, old enough to like the party. Sees the things adults explain away.

Elsewhere

The Kestrel Company — Sera Vantree, Marn Tillery, Brakk, Aldous, Edda. The last hunters to try the ruins. Half a year gone; never came home.

Osric, Hesper and Cadoc — three treasure-hunters who found something warm in the dark five centuries ago and could not put it down.

Hazel and Mabel — two green hags of the Widow Fen, sisters, who once were three.

Harrow Vane — high priest of Shar at the temple called the Iris. Has been watching Thomas for years.

Renlow Veck — a Daggerford shipping clerk with a gold signet ring.

Bramblemaw — a young green dragon who cannot explain why its eyes are wet.

Pudgy, Pinenut and Honeysuckle — squirrels.

The Dreamer — called the Green Lady by the town that never saw her. A dryad. Blond, tall, wood-and-blossom. Asleep.

Each is a piece of clear faceted stone the size of two cupped hands, and each is obviously still a heart: the chambering is there in the crystal, the great vessels sheared off clean. Stone should be cold; these are blood-warm, always. Once every few seconds they beat, and when they beat the light inside moves. They never chip, scratch or break. They have already been broken, and nothing since has been allowed to matter.

Part One

The Road South

In which four strangers leave the last friendly walls behind, a sword chooses a hand, and a village's name arrives before the village does.

Chapter IFirst Light on the Trade Way

The Silver Gauntlet stood against Daggerford's southern gatehouse, the adventurers' tavern, the last roof in town before the road. Four people sat at one of its tables who did not yet think of themselves as anything so grand as a company.

Morgash spoke first, because Morgash generally did. An orc the colour of old bone, in plate that had seen a war and did not care to discuss it — a situation I don't want to talk about right now — a caravan guard for hire who wanted one big score and then a chair by a fire somewhere for the rest of his life. Gren Greenmantle, a gnome whose beard was white and whose robes were green and gold, had met him defending a town and had healed one of his companions; it was Gren who had found the references in the old temple ledger, Gren who had drawn the map, Gren who had brought them all here. Thomas Invictus was the one the others had come for: a Daggerford native, a scribe until six months ago, a paladin of Lathander now, with a dawn on his breastplate and a psalter in his pack. His wife and his four children had been murdered by thieves he had discovered selling body parts to somebody in the south. Following that trail was what had uncovered the Broken Heart. He suspected necromancy. He wanted a name. And Jetten Elisedil, the elf, who had known Thomas since Thomas was a boy on a ranch — it's your whole life, Thomas; I'm an elf — had come to see him through it.

Morgash heard Thomas's story and swore, plainly, in front of everyone: If there is any connection, they will feel the wrath. You will have your back. Vengeance — I swear it. He would be asked to keep that oath in a way he did not expect, in a stone cell two months later, and he would keep it.

What they knew, they knew from drinks and research: the green dragon was young, a wyrmling maybe, no adult had ever been rumoured; Shar was the goddess of loss and night and forgotten memory, her faithful utterly secretive; Greenrest was a small village nobody on the road could say much about. Lord Hargrove led them to the inn's vault and they divided their stored gear and a thousand gold. Morgash took up a greatsword with a Flaming Fist duty-mark on the blade, from his old company. Then south.

The Trade Way

The road was mostly what roads are. Two "couriers" riding north — Pibb and Josk — asked the party to settle a quarrel about whether a horse needs a full night's rest, and Thomas, who knew every legitimate document-dealer in Daggerford by name, read Pibb for a forger inside a sentence; Gren, meanwhile, quietly asked the horses, who reported themselves well-fed from an apple orchard and entirely without menace. The pair huffed off north, unmasked and unhindered. A barefoot halfling priest of Chauntea named Brother Tobin blessed them all and pressed a barley-token on Jetten as a kindred spirit of the wilds, and said a thing none of them would understand for weeks:

"The lady's been strange with her gifts of late. Some fields simply cannot fail; others starve a mile off. She doesn't usually play favourites."

What the narrator knows

Tobin was orthodox enough to have already sensed the wrongness in the south, without knowing its shape. The field that cannot fail was the valley. The Earthmother was not playing favourites; she was refusing a place. The party carried his token all the way to Greenrest and never once thought to look for a dead field on the way in — and there was none to find. The valley was all bloom.

The bridge, and the cave

Late in the afternoon they came on a merchant's cart overturned on a bridge, one wheel still turning, the crates picked over, fresh blood smeared toward the riverbank. Morgash found a great many booted prints in the blood. The trail led down to an unlit cave, and Jetten went in ahead with elf eyes and heard guttural laughter — a band of hobgoblins around a fire, several of them wounded from their own ambush, a big captain among them. Gren dropped a light on poor darkvision-less Thomas, which complicated Jetten's sneaking considerably. The council was short. Jetten had no love for hobgoblins, as an elf. Thomas would not leave them to take the next traveller on the road. Jetten's first arrow opened the fight.

It was long and scrappy, the way first fights are. An archer's arrow found Morgash early and deep. Thomas's Bane settled on two of them and ruined hobgoblin aim for the rest of the night; Gren's Bless held the line up. A shaman came out of the deeper dark mid-fight with a staff of poison vine, and put Jetten nearly on the floor twice — I'm almost dead — and Jetten put himself back up with a healing word. Morgash, on a night when his dice were ice-cold, turned a miss into a blow that should have ended the captain and then, with a hobgoblin already dying on his blade, said Finally hit something. Finally. Thomas's smite lit the captain like a lamp and Jetten's arrow finished him from across the cave. Gren built a false wall out of nothing, cast from behind it, and burned the shaman down in three volleys of fire. The last warrior, surrounded, took the flat of Morgash's pommel and Gren's roared ultimatum — Surrender or die! — and dropped his sword.

The interrogation went badly for the prisoner. Who sent them? I take no orders from no one but my captain. He looked at the corpse by the fire. …I am captain now. Who had they attacked? Merchants — that was the job. Thomas wanted one more question. Jetten ended it with a shortsword through the eye: Enough, hobgoblin scum.

First Light

In the back chamber lay the hobgoblins' victims, dead barely an hour: two well-appointed guards, one of them headless, a high-elf merchant woman in Waterdhavian finery, and her human companion. Thomas knelt by the guards and knew them — paladins of Lathander, his own god. He read the psalm for the dead from his psalter, felt the warm wash of the Dawnfather's approval, and would not touch a single thing that belonged to the faithful. He noticed, too, that the Trade Way is not usually like this: the hobgoblins had seemed driven here from somewhere, scouring land they did not know.

The captain's sword was rose-gold, oddly warm, and holding a little daylight even in the dark. Thomas felt it reach for him the moment he saw it. Over the night's rest it told him its name: First Light, a Dawnbringer's blade consecrated in a temple of Lathander. He would carry it into every ruin in the Misty Forest, and lose it in the one that mattered.

What the narrator knows

Two small threads began in this cave and stayed loose. Thomas wanted one more question out of the prisoner and Jetten's blade answered first, so whatever the hobgoblins had been driven from was never learned — though Thomas had noticed it was strange, and said so. And the letter, addressed in a careful hand to someone in Daggerford, rode in Jetten's pack for the rest of the campaign, unopened. The high-elf merchant was somebody in Waterdeep, and somebody was waiting for her. It is a kindness the road never asked of them, and it is still there to be done.

They buried the victims in shallow sword-dug graves with last rites, having briefly considered staking hobgoblin heads along the road as a warning and decided against it, and slept in a clean corner of the cave. Among the recovered goods was a letter, unsealed, addressed to someone in Daggerford. Jetten pocketed it unread. Nobody ever opened it. In the morning, to the east, stood the treeline of the Misty Forest, and the map said to leave the road there.

Chapter IIThe Lure in the Mist

Thomas pressed Gren about the village before they set out. The gnome had grown up in these woods and had never heard of Greenrest — but Misty Forest settlements are small, secluded and private, and a village unknown even to locals would not be strange. Morgash hauled the wrecked cart off the road before leaving, because it was in the way and he was tidy. Then east, off the map's last safe line.

The forest was Gren's childhood and Jetten's element, and the day's running joke was that neither of them could find their way through it. Trails a little more than deer trails, mist everywhere, small animals watching; Jetten scouted ahead and the ranger and the native gnome wandered, while the Baldur's Gate city orc, who had never been in anything like this, kept pointing Jetten the right direction and was right every time. By noon they knew they were halfway through.

The clearing

They stopped to rest in a clearing and the birdsong stopped with them. The mist went still. Jetten and Gren both read it in time, and neither was surprised when five blights pulled themselves out of the treeline — three of twig, two of vine — and ringed the camp. It was over quickly and nobody took a scratch: Jetten killed a vine blight before it ever moved; Morgash halved a twig blight in one slice and found a new trick in himself, a rush of adrenaline that carried him across the clearing to bottle up the far side; Thomas laughed off an entangling vine — you will never be able to catch me — and split the last twig blight with a stroke that found the perfect seam; Gren named his fire, and the woody thing burned twice as well for it. Afterward Gren stood over the ash and said what he would keep saying for the rest of the campaign: These are a corruption of life itself. There's more corruption somewhere. We need to find its source. Thomas opened his senses for fey, fiend and undead and found nothing within reach.

What the narrator knows

The blight-source was never found, and it was never in the valley. The valley was the one place in the Misty Forest with no corruption in it at all — only grief, which is different. The fog country around it was the same fey, foggy wild it had always been. Gren was right that something was wrong in the woods. He was looking on the wrong side of the tree-line.

The light in the mist

Hours more east, and late in the afternoon the mist gave way to bare wet rock, a low grey cliff split by a cleft taller than a man — and a single small light, round and warm as a candle, casting no glow and no shadow, drifting toward them and back. Quicker, then insistent, the way a hand beckons someone who is running out of time. Almost pleading. It slipped into the cave.

Gren, who is kind to fairies, thought one of them might be in distress. Jetten did not think this was Greenrest but was willing to take a quick look. Inside: cold air over the ankles, wet stone, something sweetly rotten. Gren dropped his light on Thomas again. Jetten crept ahead and saw the ambush waiting in the webs before it sprang. How delightful. It was a trap, guys. The dancing light winked out.

What followed was a fight in waves in the dark of a ruined, web-strung hall. Jetten went in blades-first on the ettercap that had lured them and it clawed him and let out a long wail that called the brood. Gren answered with three rays of fire and burned the first one to a crisp; Morgash — whose turn came last, having rolled the worst initiative anyone would see all campaign, minutes after his all-day navigation clinic — read the ettercap's darting looks at a shut door and dashed across the hall to block it. Then the door burst. The Broodmother came through over him, bit deep and poisoned him, and Morgash, poisoned and surrounded, turned a miss into a hit with sheer precision and the patented double back-swing, and swallowed a dose of Keoghtom's ointment to burn the poison out. Broodlings webbed Thomas to the floor; the will-o'-wisp came back, and this time it looked very hostile, and shocked Gren from nowhere and vanished again. Gren stopped trying to hit a thing he could not see and turned his unerring darts of force on the Broodmother instead. Jetten peppered her. Thomas cut himself free and split a broodling clean out of existence. And Gren's last volley of magic missiles dropped the Broodmother where she stood.

The wisp never came back. It fled, not slain. Gren, who had been tricked, made a vow about it and all its kind, and would keep the vow for the rest of his life without ever getting to spend it.

The ruined temple

The arena revealed itself in the calm afterward: an ancient elven shrine, webbed and bone-strewn, that the ettercaps had made a feeding hall. Thomas's temple schooling read the stones — statues to fey figures, alms bowls that once held water, a small temple as elven temples are. Gren quietly poured a little water into each empty bowl before burning the webs out. Down a south passage lay a dead fey creature, an elf perhaps, months gone, a dagger still on the body, beside the ettercaps' victim-loot.

What the narrator knows

The ruined temple held more than loot. There was a line carved into the lintel — a light, and a road home — that Jetten's glance read only as etchings worn past reading, and beneath the shrine a thin place into the Feywild, which two of Thomas's checks came within a point of finding and did not. That thin place was where the displacer pack had come through. The dead elf in the south passage, months gone with a dagger still on the body, was Goldthorn's last bearer; nobody examined him past a glance. The watchtower above Greenrest, offered plainly as a place to look at the valley from before walking into it, was never climbed. None of it mattered to the ending. All of it was a door left quietly shut by people who had a map and a good reason to hurry, and a story that would have been happy to open any of them.

From the pile came two things that would matter. The first was a gilded scimitar that wanted to speak to Jetten the way First Light had spoken to Thomas — Goldthorn, lost with its bearer to the webs of a hidden temple in the Misty Forest and dormant since, whose fey magic could stir once between dawns into a bloom of clinging faerie light that let nothing hide in shadow. Jetten attuned to it overnight. The second was a stoppered clay vial of bitter green cordial among the web-wrapped effects of a traveller who had never left, its label in a tidy careful hand: One swallow, seventh-day, as ever. — Selma. Thomas read aloud what could be learned of it: whoever drank the swallow slept utterly, without dreams, for seven nights. No apothecary could name any other virtue, nor say why anyone would brew such a thing.

The village's name had found them before the village did. They also took a lantern whose light, they would learn, reveals what does not wish to be seen.

They slept in the cleansed temple, and two hours east the next morning the trees thinned, the trail climbed a last rise, and the forest let them go. Below, cradled in green meadows under a cloudless sky: slate roofs snug around a square, straight chimney smoke, orchards and hedged fields, birdsong, cut hay, woodsmoke. An old stone watchtower on the height to the right. And dead centre in all that green, a single great tree gone grey and sickly — the one wrong note in an otherwise perfect song.

Part Two

The Green Valley

In which a town welcomes them like family, four sleepers dream the same woman, and three treasure-hunters five centuries dead ask them to stay.

Chapter IIINo Crying in Greenrest

At the trailhead where the path left the forest, Morgash found animal prints circling — two legs, four, six, eight, impossible to say — that turned back into the trees. Whatever it was, it would not approach the town. He said so out loud. Then they went down.

Orchards, flourishing fields, woodsmoke, prosperity; it felt richer than Daggerford. A crowd had gathered before they reached the square, and Mayor Oswin Applewhite came through it to greet them like family coming home. He walked them through the whole of it himself — the Long Rest, where Ambrose kept the inn; the smithy; the church of the Earthmother; his own tower up the hill (the mayor always lives at the top, it's tradition); the square; the brew house; Selma's apothecary; the dockside on the lake; the Full Larder, where Mother Wend already knew all four of their names; the Hedgewatch garrison, fifty strong. Lodging was free. Food was free. Ale was free. We love our guests. You don't have to worry about a penny here.

Over supper at the Long Rest the mayor told them what he knew of the Broken Heart, which was what the last visitors had told him: a relic, four ruins, a company of adventurers called the Kestrels who had come through some months back looking for it and never come back. Sadly, life is good here, he said. As long as everything is status quo, I'm happy. And then, as a boast, the line the whole campaign would turn on: There is no crying in Greenrest.

Constable Warin Holt cut in on the monster talk, and later, quietly: I think I know the mistakes those adventurers made. Come to the barracks. I don't want to bother Oswin with such things. At the barracks he was plain. His scouts had tracked displacer beasts in the forest for years. They had stalked the Kestrels, waited for a weak moment, and killed them at an old temple south-east of town; the scouts had seen the battle-sign and would not go closer. They've latched onto you like they did the others. They cannot be deterred until their quarry is slain. Kill them first, at a moment of your choosing, at full strength — or come home wounded some night and be jumped. His men defended the town only. We don't leave the town, and they don't come in here.

On the inn porch a halfling child named Pip informed Gren that he was taller than him, buddy, and then, unprompted: Did the black cats follow you too? Big black cats with too many legs, seen circling the town when the Kestrels were here and again an hour before this party came out of the trees. He had never told anyone, because he didn't want to bother them. He refused a gold piece on the grounds that he had everything he needed here. And Thomas found the gravedigger, Coll, at the inn — a lifelong local who said it hasn't been right since the Kestrels came through complaining they weren't sleeping well, which had reminded him of being a boy with bad dreams before Selma helped him out. The whole town took the sleep-draught weekly, he confirmed, from childhood — as soon as we're old enough to dream. Thomas took out the vial from the ettercap temple and read Selma's label to the table. Same seventh-day cadence.

It was Gren who decided it. Let's spend the night without drinking the tonic. Something's fishy about this town.

The night of dreams

All four dreamed the same figure, and each of them dreamed her differently. Thomas dreamed the murder of his wife and children, and underneath the oath of vengeance found the grief he had built the oath to cover. Morgash dreamed Lae'zel's goodbye in Baldur's Gate — I cannot love someone in this world — and the red dragon that carried her away. Gren dreamed the village elders sending him north, and the suspicion, never confirmed, that it was the fire in him they were sending away: cast out, unwanted, unloved. Jetten dreamed his sister, dead so long ago, and the cruelty of outliving her — and while he dreamed it, eyes open in trance, the moonstone at his throat glowed bright green.

In the morning they compared notes, except for Morgash, who admitted the weeping woman and refused the details. I won't give details about my broken heart. Gren's temple schooling surfaced with perfect clarity: the figure was a dryad — fey, immortal, a life rotated between elf-shape and tree. Thomas connected it at once. The dead tree in the square.

What the narrator knows

Anyone who slept in Greenrest dreamed of grief, except the villagers, who had taken the tonic weekly for generations and no longer dreamed at all. A private dream belongs to the dreamer; a dream four people can compare at breakfast is someone else's, reaching out. The town had solved this mystery centuries ago by not thinking about it. Nobody in Greenrest had withheld the tonic from the party. It simply never occurred to anyone to press their grandmother's habit on a traveller.

In the square that morning, four watchmen stood posted on the tree. Their orders came from the constable and the mayor: let no one destroy it, let no one chip the bark, watch it. It had always been like this, a guard said, born here; they kept trying to revive it and nothing worked. Jetten looked at it and saw a dead tree. The Count

Chapter VThe Long Rest

They walked out of the Hollow into a silence that told them the place was spent. Thomas could not carry his own shield; the party carried it, and Morgash found him a stick for a cane. What the narrator knows

There has never been a cleric in Greenrest. No child of the valley has ever knelt and received the Weave. The first dream

The town day

Chauntea had forgiven the town. Greenrest had a healer of its own.

Morgash found that ten thousand gold in gems was the big score after all. Warin Holt asked him to hold the gate at Hedgewatch, and he stayed — I feel responsible — and in time the town forgave him, and Oswin always stood him breakfast and dinner, and he had a permanent room at the Full Larder.

Jetten stayed friends with Thomas and came and went. One evening the moonstone at his throat glowed green for the last time, and he had a real dream of his sister, sent by a fey who owed him, and he said goodbye to her properly. In the morning the stone was milky white.

Thomas went home. A month later in Daggerford he mended things with the temple of Lathander and walked to the five graves in the walled yard, and read every name aloud, and then his own, and stayed until it was dark. The grief ended there — not the memory, the grief. He became a paladin again, properly, and went wandering with Jetten for some years after, the two of them, slaying evil where they found it.

Gren learned that every squirrel in the Misty Forest knew that Gren the Mighty had freed the Green Lady, and his warren, which had sent him away, sent for him, and there was a feast. He came back to Greenrest afterward and planted the Everseed Acorn in the square beside the old tree, and stayed. It grew into a young oak that drops its leaves every autumn, as an oak should. Pudgy became the prophet of a small religion whose members reluctantly tolerate Morgash. Gren's old debt to the orc is paid in acorns, one by the bedside every morning; Morgash steps on them about half the time.

Nobody was wrong enough to make it clean. They freed a soul a thousand years caged, and they turned off a village's good fortune in front of the village, and both of those things are true. Somewhere past the fog, Corin's story ended a long time ago, and nobody will ever know how. But she stopped waiting for it, which is the only ending anyone gets.

AfterwordWhat the Narrator Knew

A campaign about a grief nobody could see is also, unavoidably, a campaign about what four people chose to look at. These are the narrator's notes gathered in one place — what the company saw, and what stayed just out of view.

What they did well

Credit first, because it is real, and because there is more of it than of anything else. The party found the campaign's opening clues faster than the campaign expected them to. They read Selma's label aloud before they had ever seen the village. They chose, on their first night, to sleep without the tonic — the single most important decision anyone made in nine sessions, and it was made from suspicion alone. Gren named the dryad from a dream on the first morning, and Thomas connected her to the tree in the square inside a minute. Morgash read the displacer prints at the trailhead and said so out loud, and read the bullywugs' glowing eyes before an arrow flew.

They were as good with people as with prints. Morgash gave Oswin the honest version at a breakfast that was going wrong, and mended it. Jetten kept a portrait back from a sale on a hunch, carried it to a shopkeeper's counter, and it produced the name Thomas had crossed a forest for. Morgash held a friend back from a murder with his arms and an argument, and when the friend chose anyway, stayed his friend. On the lakeshore, with a goddess's last offer on the table and every resurrection in the world in the balance, four people talked a fifth back from it in ten minutes of plain speech. They freed her at the cost of everything the Heart could have given them — and spent the first Thornheart dawn on a squirrel, without being asked twice. Of every door in the last session, that one was the smallest, and it is the one that says most about who they were.

Threads for the next story

A good campaign leaves more behind than it uses. These are the ones still lying in the road, for anyone who goes back that way.

The Traveller's Letter — addressed in a careful hand to someone in Daggerford, still sealed in Jetten's pack. Somebody in Waterdeep is waiting for a merchant who will not come home.

The fey temple — a line on the lintel, a light, and a road home, and beneath the shrine a thin place the displacer pack came through. The watchtower above the valley overlooks both roads to it.

Warin's barracks — "I don't want to bother Oswin with such things." The constable's fuller theory of the Kestrels' end is still his to tell.

Coll's empty plot, and Tess by the tannery — a grave dug for people who never came home, and a woman in Daggerford who never got Marn's letter. Both are one visit from closed.

Hesper's word — the one question nobody put to a five-hundred-year-old woman: Greenrest? She would have said she did not know it, and half a campaign's theory would have gone quiet.

The Widow Fen — a drop-stone on the moor path, a jar marked ours in a lower room, and an annotated Volo's Guide nobody opened.

The Iris — a shut stone eye at the centre of a temple of the Lady of Loss, passed twice with a prisoner and a priest more urgent than a carving. Above Harrow Vane there is always somebody.

The Daggerford ledger office — where a scribe once started pulling threads, and where Veck's other customers may still keep books.

The Wyrmwood — rooms north of the hall unexplored, a druid's story untold, and a black sword in the ground on the lakeshore that has not stopped calling.

Corin — a name said aloud in the first two dreams and never followed. What became of him past the fog is the one thing nobody in this story ever learned. A ballad sheet on the roadside remembers him; so, perhaps, does a raven-haired family somewhere.

None of what stayed shut changed the ending. Four hunters came down the Trade Way after a relic and left having given it away; they were asked, twice in one morning, for opposite things by people they liked, and they chose the harder one and lived with the square's silence afterward. A mystery is the sum of what its detectives pick up, and the thing these four picked up, in the end, was a stranger's thousand-year grief, and they carried it home. That is the part worth keeping.

What If…?

The last session was written with six doors in it. The company opened one. These are the other five, as they were prepared — what each would have cost, and what it would have made of each of the four.

Appendix AWhat If…?

Both roads — hers and Shar's — needed all four shards at the tree, and every holder had a veto: Morgash held the Graveheart, Gren the Tideheart, Jetten the Duskheart, Invictus the Thornheart. Nobody could do either thing alone, and nobody could take a shard by force. Whatever the company chose, it had to be chosen by talking. That was the design, and it is why the argument on the lakeshore was the scene the whole campaign had been building toward.

A · Free her — the road taken

All four hearts into the knot, one at a time, each turning a quarter of the tree gold, with the town objecting in the square as it did. The Dreamer steps out, the crystal gives, and she goes. The Heart goes with her; nobody gets a resurrection. The leaves fall; the fog returns to the valley's rim; Enid's prayer is answered. This is the ending the company chose and the one the chronicle tells, and each of the four's fate is in the Epilogue.

B · Midnight into the knot

Shar's road. Invictus drives the black sword into the heart knot with the four shards at the tree; the blade goes in as if the wood had been waiting for it; the warmth goes out of the bark, out of the shards, and up the steel into his hand. The shards go grey and cold. The blossoms close. The tree stays green, and stops. When he pulls Midnight out it is heavier than it was, and it beats. The Dreamer is trapped under the square forever. The town, which heard her ask, chooses its summer anyway; nobody fights, nobody dies, and Oswin cries with relief and cannot look at the tree. There is a feast, a key to the town, and Invictus is named a friend of Greenrest.

Invictus. A month later, in Daggerford, five graves in a row. He draws Midnight and says their names one at a time, and the ground gives them back. Ann calls him Thomas, the way she always did; he can say all five of their names and still cannot say his own. One morning the youngest asks if he remembers the day he dropped the whole jug of syrup on the kitchen floor, and everyone laughs, and he laughs with them, and he does not remember it, and never will. There are a lot of days like that. But they are at his table, all five of them, and most days that is enough.

Morgash. Word travels — the dragon, the temple, the choice at the tree. Months later a shadow crosses the sun wherever he has ended up: a red dragon, and on its back, Lae'zel. I said I could not love anyone in this world. I have been at war since. I was wrong about you. You can make the tough choices and the sacrifices to save a people when they need to be made. There is a place at my side, my Morgash. I need you. Ride with me. Whether he goes is his to decide. The offer is real, and it is the one thing he wanted more than the chair by the fire.

Gren. The squirrels stop coming. Not one in the whole Wyrmwood will come down a tree for him now. Word reaches the warren and the elders send word back: don't come home. It is the second time they have cast him out, and this time the forest agrees with them; he sealed a fey creature into a tree for good, and nothing that lives under leaves forgets that. The Everseed Acorn went grey in his pocket the day the sword went in. He cannot plant it anywhere now.

Jetten. He does not come to the feast. By the time anyone thinks to look for him he is on the Trade Way going south, and he finds new company, the way elves do eventually. He does not write, and when someone mentions Thomas he changes the subject. The moonstone never glows again. His last words to Invictus, if there is a goodbye at all: You got five. She didn't get one. He means his sister.

Greenrest. The summer never turns. There is no autumn, ever again, and the children grow up without seeing a leaf fall. The town ties harvest ribbons around the scar in the knot. The dreams stop, and Greenrest sleeps well for the first time in a generation. Selma pours the tonic out, and nobody hears her anymore.

C · Walk away with the shards

The road that costs nothing and gives nothing. The shards can only be joined at the knot, so keeping them means keeping four separate stones, each with its own small power, and no resurrections for anyone. The company leaves Greenrest with the four hearts in their packs. The town keeps its summer. The tree keeps its buds and never quite blooms. She is not sealed, only left — and wherever they sleep, every night, she asks again. Someday someone else could carry the shards home.

Invictus. Carries the Thornheart and a sword he cannot use. The Duskheart's voice is quiet now — Shar called it her last offer and meant it — but the buried question is not: he crossed a forest for a name, found it, took it, and walked away from both the thing that could have brought his family back and the thing that could have set a stranger free. He has the pain again, undimmed. What he does with it is the whole of the rest of his life.

Morgash. Retires on the dragon's gems, as he always meant to, and finds the chair by the fire is not what he thought. The Graveheart on its cord under his shirt beats a little harder some nights than others, and he knows exactly what it is asking. He held a gate once against a netherbrain. This time he walked away from one.

Gren. The squirrels still come — he did nothing to the Green Lady, after all — but they ask about her, in the way squirrels ask, every time. The acorn stays green in his pocket and he never finds the place to plant it, because the one place that fit was the one he left with her still weeping under it. Pudgy follows him regardless.

Jetten. Sleeps in trance with the Duskheart on his chest and dreams her asking, every night, in his sister's cadence. He was the one who said it's not right to let her remain enslaved, and he was the one who could have made it happen by putting one stone in a knot. The moonstone glows every night, faintly, and he stops looking at it.

Greenrest. Unchanged, which is the point. Lucky, kind, comfortable, dreaming a little now that the tonic has failed, and never quite sure what four strangers took away with them in their packs.

D · The company cannot agree

No blades between friends, ever. Both roads need all four shards, so nobody can do either alone, and each holder decides what happens to their own stone. If even one holder will not give theirs up, neither road can be walked, and it ends as C — but the argument is the scene. The one the campaign was built for. On the morning it was played, the split was real: Invictus leaning Shar's way with the sword whispering, Jetten on record against, Gren and Morgash undeclared. It resolved on the lakeshore in about ten minutes of honest talk. Had it not:

Invictus holds the Thornheart, the only stone that can raise the dead on its own. He can keep his veto and keep a squirrel's worth of resurrection every dawn, and nothing else. The friends who held him back in the cell cannot hold his shard; they can only ask.

Morgash holds the Graveheart and has said out loud, twice, you will regret this. He is the one who told Oswin the honest version at breakfast. In a stalemate he is the one most likely to name the price plainly: a woman under the square, or a town above it, and which of us gets to decide for the other.

Gren holds the Tideheart and the standing that comes from being the one person in the company the forest trusts. If the split hardens, he is the one who can walk away from Greenrest and still be welcome under leaves. He is also the one who said if you do this, you will need redemption, and would have to decide whether to keep saying it.

Jetten holds the Duskheart, which is a veto and a temptation in one — see E. He is the company's conscience and Thomas's oldest friend, and in a true deadlock those two things pull opposite ways.

Greenrest waits. The tree keeps its buds. The elders meet. And every night four sleepers in the Long Rest are asked the same question by someone who cannot stop asking it.

E · Jetten uses the Duskheart on Invictus

Shar's own shard reaches into a memory once at dusk and changes it. The offer was less than a day old and lasted under ten minutes; Jetten could have erased it or rewritten it, and Invictus would have had one chance to resist. The elf who spoke Shar's words takes them back with Shar's own stone. If it works, Invictus forgets the offer entirely; Shar called it her last and meant it, and Midnight goes quiet. The company remembers what they did to him. He does not.

Invictus walks to the tree with a clear head he did not earn and a sword that has stopped talking. He frees her, probably, and it is a kindness — and the kindness is Shar's, delivered through his best friend's hands, at no cost to the goddess: the memory that hurts, taken. He is the campaign's proof that oblivion-as-mercy works. Somewhere he knows a piece is missing, the way he knew about his name.

Morgash would have argued against it — he is the one who wants Thomas to choose, and said so with his arms around the man's chest — and, outvoted, would have carried it. He does not forget things. He would look at Thomas at the tree and see a man who had been decided for, and think of the Basilisk Gate, and the nine who lived because someone else made the call.

Gren said Shar was the deepest of evils, and here is her shard doing her work on a friend. He would have wanted to be told it was necessary, and would not quite have believed it, and would have watched the acorn in his pocket for a week afterward to see whether it stayed green.

Jetten pays the price the shard was built to extract. He said it's the evil trying to work its way into you, and then used the evil's own instrument on the person he had known longest. The moonstone's last glow, in this ending, is not a gift from a grateful fey; it is Sehanine, lady of dreams, asking her devotee what he did to a man's memory.

Greenrest gets the same morning as A — the leaves, the fog, Enid's answered prayer — and never knows the difference. That is the whole trouble with it.

F · Raise Pudgy before they leave — the road taken

The Thornheart raises one body a dawn, dead ten days or less, and Invictus held it. Three squirrels lay in the great hall, one of them wearing Longshadow's feather; Honeysuckle and Pinenut would each have needed a dawn of their own, and it had to happen before any Shar's-road ending, since a drained shard raises no one. The company spent the very first raising on Pudgy, without being asked twice. Did we win? Where's Honeysuckle? They buried the other two under a tree with acorns, and a squirrel became a prophet. Of all the doors in the last session, this was the smallest, and it is the one that tells you most about who they were.

Perytons are monstrous predators that hunt people—particularly humans and elves—in favor of all other prey. Bullywugs are frog folk of the deep fens, touched by the Feywild and convinced — each and every one — of their own tremendous importance. Animated Objects

—a welcome nobody should accept

Spellcasters of sufficient craft can pour a sliver of will into the furnishings of their homes, and the result is a household that never sleeps and never stops working. An animated object has no mind of its own — only its instructions, followed with the patience of a thing that has never once been tired.

Most of the time they are indistinguishable from what they appear to be. A suit of armor stands its post for years; a broom sweeps the same floor at the same hour; a rug lies flat by the hearth, exactly where a guest would warm their feet. Green Hags

—the party's own research,

Green hags are fey crones who make their homes in drowned and rotting places — bogs, fens, and swamps where the water is black and the paths are few. They delight in misery the way gardeners delight in growing things: they cultivate it, harvest it, trade in it. A green hag rarely opens with violence when a bargain will hurt more; her prices sound survivable and never are.

They are consummate deceivers. A green hag can throw her voice across the water, mimic the cry of a drowning man or the voice of someone dearly missed, and walk unseen when it suits her. What looks like a helpless figure on a distant hummock is often the hag herself, fishing.

Sharrans

Chapter VII · the Iris — Harrow Vane, high priest; zealots, enforcers and acolytes

The Faithful of the Lady of Loss

She does not ask you to love the dark. Only to set down what you are carrying.

—the offer, made in
a thousand towns

Shar is the Lady of Loss, mistress of night, secrets, and forgetting. Her clergy recruit with relief rather than threats: they look for people in the worst part of a grief and offer to take the memory away. Most of the faithful came to her exactly that way and remember the bargain as a kindness. A Sharran is rarely a fanatic. He is usually somebody who was helped, and stayed.

On that mercy the church builds a doctrine. A grief allowed to run its course is spent and gone; a grief kept open never runs dry. So the order does not heal sorrow — it cultivates it, in itself and in others, and counts the work holy. Sharran houses are built underground or inside old ruins, keep to night hours, and are generally tolerated by neighbours who have no idea what they are.

The faithful are living folk, never undead; Shar's church has no use for corpses. Its ranks run roughly four ways. Acolytes are the newest and do most of the casting — darkness, holding words, guardian spirits. Zealots are the devout rank and file, plainly equipped and hard to rout. Enforcers are trained soldiers in the order's pay, armed with polearms and blades, who fight like veterans and tend to pick the weakest figure in a line and stay on it. Priests are few and dangerous out of all proportion to their number: necrotic magic spent freely, authority over a whole house, and spells enough to simply leave a fight going badly.

They talk before they draw, and the offer is sincere. Refusing it does not offend them — patience costs the church nothing, and the offer will be made again. They take prisoners for purposes they do not explain. They are hard to intimidate, since a person whose worst memory has already been taken has little left to threaten. And they answer upward, always, to somebody the traveller has not met.

Giant Crocodile

Chapter VIII · the Wyrmwood's flooded hall

Ambush Hunters of Deep Water

A giant crocodile is a crocodile grown to enormous size: a huge, thick-hided beast that lumbers on land but swims far faster than it walks, and can stay beneath the water for an hour at a stretch.

It fights with jaws and tail. The bite clamps down and holds on — anything up to the size of a horse is seized and pinned, able to do little but struggle while the crocodile keeps it. The tail sweeps smaller creatures clean off their feet.

Giant crocodiles are animals, not monsters: hungry and patient rather than cruel. Green Dragons

Chapter VIII · Bramblemaw

Dragons of Deceit and Derision

From forbidden forest depths, green dragons whisper evils into the world and manipulate the lives of those who listen. Elusive, conniving, and egotistical, these chromatic dragons patiently prey on the fears of shorter-lived beings, corrupting and isolating them. Green dragons might lurk amid labyrinthine wildernesses for centuries without revealing themselves; even their most devoted followers might know them only as the voice of the woodlands or a whisper in their dreams.

Despite their might, most green dragons disdain physical violence, viewing combat as servants’ work and preferring to trick foes into dangerous or exploitative scenarios. These dragons collect “baubles” that embody their webs of manipulation and serve as tools of extortion, such as compromising documents, family heirlooms, and sentimental treasures.
