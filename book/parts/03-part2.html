Part Two

The Green Valley

In which a town welcomes them like family, four sleepers dream the same woman, and three treasure-hunters five centuries dead ask them to stay.

Chapter IIINo Crying in Greenrest

At the trailhead where the path left the forest, Morgash found animal prints circling — two legs, four, six, eight, impossible to say — that turned back into the trees. Whatever it was, it would not approach the town. He said so out loud. Then they went down.

Orchards, flourishing fields, woodsmoke, prosperity; it felt richer than Daggerford. A crowd had gathered before they reached the square, and Mayor Oswin Applewhite came through it to greet them like family coming home. He walked them through the whole of it himself — the Long Rest, where Ambrose kept the inn; the smithy; the church of the Earthmother; his own tower up the hill (the mayor always lives at the top, it's tradition); the square; the brew house; Selma's apothecary; the dockside on the lake; the Full Larder, where Mother Wend already knew all four of their names; the Hedgewatch garrison, fifty strong. Lodging was free. Food was free. Ale was free. We love our guests. You don't have to worry about a penny here.

Over supper at the Long Rest the mayor told them what he knew of the Broken Heart, which was what the last visitors had told him: a relic, four ruins, a company of adventurers called the Kestrels who had come through some months back looking for it and never come back. Sadly, life is good here, he said. As long as everything is status quo, I'm happy. And then, as a boast, the line the whole campaign would turn on: There is no crying in Greenrest.

Constable Warin Holt cut in on the monster talk, and later, quietly: I think I know the mistakes those adventurers made. Come to the barracks. I don't want to bother Oswin with such things. At the barracks he was plain. His scouts had tracked displacer beasts in the forest for years. They had stalked the Kestrels, waited for a weak moment, and killed them at an old temple south-east of town; the scouts had seen the battle-sign and would not go closer. They've latched onto you like they did the others. They cannot be deterred until their quarry is slain. Kill them first, at a moment of your choosing, at full strength — or come home wounded some night and be jumped. His men defended the town only. We don't leave the town, and they don't come in here.

On the inn porch a halfling child named Pip informed Gren that he was taller than him, buddy, and then, unprompted: Did the black cats follow you too? Big black cats with too many legs, seen circling the town when the Kestrels were here and again an hour before this party came out of the trees. He had never told anyone, because he didn't want to bother them. He refused a gold piece on the grounds that he had everything he needed here. And Thomas found the gravedigger, Coll, at the inn — a lifelong local who said it hasn't been right since the Kestrels came through complaining they weren't sleeping well, which had reminded him of being a boy with bad dreams before Selma helped him out. The whole town took the sleep-draught weekly, he confirmed, from childhood — as soon as we're old enough to dream. Thomas took out the vial from the ettercap temple and read Selma's label to the table. Same seventh-day cadence.

It was Gren who decided it. Let's spend the night without drinking the tonic. Something's fishy about this town.

The night of dreams

All four dreamed the same figure, and each of them dreamed her differently. Thomas dreamed the murder of his wife and children, and underneath the oath of vengeance found the grief he had built the oath to cover. Morgash dreamed Lae'zel's goodbye in Baldur's Gate — I cannot love someone in this world — and the red dragon that carried her away. Gren dreamed the village elders sending him north, and the suspicion, never confirmed, that it was the fire in him they were sending away: cast out, unwanted, unloved. Jetten dreamed his sister, dead so long ago, and the cruelty of outliving her — and while he dreamed it, eyes open in trance, the moonstone at his throat glowed bright green.

In the morning they compared notes, except for Morgash, who admitted the weeping woman and refused the details. I won't give details about my broken heart. Gren's temple schooling surfaced with perfect clarity: the figure was a dryad — fey, immortal, a life rotated between elf-shape and tree. Thomas connected it at once. The dead tree in the square.

What the narrator knows

Anyone who slept in Greenrest dreamed of grief, except the villagers, who had taken the tonic weekly for generations and no longer dreamed at all. A private dream belongs to the dreamer; a dream four people can compare at breakfast is someone else's, reaching out. The town had solved this mystery centuries ago by not thinking about it. Nobody in Greenrest had withheld the tonic from the party. It simply never occurred to anyone to press their grandmother's habit on a traveller.

In the square that morning, four watchmen stood posted on the tree. Their orders came from the constable and the mayor: let no one destroy it, let no one chip the bark, watch it. It had always been like this, a guard said, born here; they kept trying to revive it and nothing worked. Jetten looked at it and saw a dead tree. Thomas looked harder and saw a sick one, uncared-for, not quite gone. Morgash said, loudly, that four guards for a dead tree did not add up. Gren said what they were all thinking: It might be her tree. And Selma's drugging the whole town. We talk to the apothecary after.

They did not talk to the apothecary after. The hunt took the day.

What the narrator knows

This is the morning the party made up its mind about Greenrest, and it is worth being exact about what it was made up from: a label on a vial, a gravedigger's childhood memory, four guards on a tree, and one night of bad dreams. From those four facts Gren produced Selma's drugging the whole town, and the theory never really left the table. Every one of those facts was true; the conclusion was not, which is the ordinary way a good theory goes wrong. Selma was an honest woman brewing her grandmother's recipe; the guards were reverence and prudence; the town had no idea what it was sitting on. What the party had actually found was a village that did not dream in a valley that gave strangers nightmares — a strangeness in plain sight, and an innocent one. They read a conspiracy into an absence, as anyone might. Warin's whisper — I don't want to bother Oswin with such things — was never followed up at the barracks; it was only the constable's embarrassment about fifty men who could not leave the town, but that, too, would have been worth hearing him say.

The Kestrels' camp

Warin's line ran south-east to a ruined temple in the woods: a cold firepit, corpses, a chest, a debris pile — the Kestrel Company's last camp. Jetten's eyes picked the first displacer beast out of the fog at a hundred and thirty-five feet, prowling, watching, waiting for them to move. His opening shot slid off the thing's shimmer as if it had never been there, and it howled, and two more came, and then the alpha.

The first round was an education. The beasts stood a step to one side of where they appeared to stand; their tentacles reached further than anyone expected; Gren's thunder washed over them and did nothing at all; even Jetten's faerie fire, the textbook answer, found one beast that shrugged it off. A tentacle crit put Thomas at three hit points and broke his blessing. Jetten was mauled and dropped, and Gren's quiet cure put him back on his feet. Morgash dashed the courtyard, ate the alpha's rends, drank three potions, hit the floor and refused it — his orcish blood stood him up at one hit point — and then killed the first beast with a backswing.

Then Thomas remembered the lantern. Unhooded, its clear light filled thirty feet, and inside that circle the beasts' after-images tore off them like smoke — they shrieked and raged and, being clever animals, immediately learned the rule and skirmished at the edge of the glow. Thomas swore a vow of enmity, laid a bane on one that could not shake it, and when he hit the second beast with a thunderous smite that hurled it ten feet backward, Morgash's opportunity strike killed it in the air. Gren was the artillery and the medic both, wand and spell and unerring dart all night, because a thing that cannot be seen cannot dodge magic that does not miss. Jetten marked target after target and landed a dagger strike so perfect it went in to the hilt. And the alpha died on Thomas's turn — First Light through the chest, Lathander's light exploding out of its back. Leaderless, the last beast fled into the forest and was never seen again.

The camp held two caches. A crate stamped Full Larder — jars of Greenrest preserves, healing potions, a silver locket with a kestrel on it, a charcoal sketch of a weeping woman, a journal in the chronicler Marn Tillery's hand, and Sera Vantree's map of the four ruins. And the company's own gear in a brush pile: Sera's bow, a maul that never stopped moving, a pearl of power Gren had wanted and could not afford in Wend's shop that morning (glad I didn't buy it), and a suit of plate armour with claw-rents through the breast that had belonged to a shield-bearer named Brakk. They walked back to Greenrest for a safe night, and at the table that night they chose: the caves. The one the townsfolk had pointed the Kestrels toward.

What the narrator knows

The pack had come through a thin place at the ruined fey temple deep in the woods — the very shrine the party had cleaned the webs out of. The town had known about it all along; Warin's scouts had tracked it for years, and even Pip had watched it from the hill and counted too many legs. Knowing was all Greenrest could ever do about it. When the party killed it, the welcome, already warm, became open gratitude, and the party became the heroes of the valley. It was the high-water mark. The rest of the campaign would fall from it.

Chapter IVThe Heart Under the Hill

They never went back to town first. From the Kestrels' camp they read Marn's journal again — It is no shrine. It is a catacomb, cut deep under the hill, the dead laid in rows in the walls. And they did not stay in them — and Morgash made the argument that carried: the Kestrels never got past the dead, so the relic was probably still in there, and this time they knew exactly what was waiting. Thomas said aloud that the shrine was the farthest of the four from Greenrest and that he was nervous about the hags and the temple of Shar. They slept at the camp and went down the shrine road in the morning.

A minaret stuck out of the forest floor with a stair going down inside it. Gren asked a squirrel about it first. No, I don't go down there. That's not where the food is. Weird sounds, weird noises. We don't go down there. He fed it more rations than it could carry anyway and asked it to come and find him if it saw bad guys. That was useless, he said afterward. They never give you any information. Then Gren lit the sun on Thomas's shield, and they went down the spiral.

A statue of living rock at the bottom, robed, taller than a person, worn past recognition — no face, no hands — with an offering basin at its feet. A skeleton impaled on floor spikes that were not part of the original stone. Thomas read the place with a scholar's eye and found nothing he knew: no holy symbol, no scripture, no iconography of any living faith. Five hundred years old and not a scratch on it. The chisel-work was wrong for any tool still in use, far too many small strokes — the work of people with nothing but time. Whoever cut this had not been following a tradition. They were making it up. And the basin had never held an offering. Whoever built it had never brought anything to it.

The Hollowing

The catacombs were two doors into the same crypt, and skeletons came out of the first one. They were nothing. Morgash's maul shattered one on the riposte; Jetten's arrow exploded another into pieces. They opened every coffin in the walls afterward and every one was empty, and Gren said a prayer over each.

Behind the next door stood a dead man in the centre of a room of zombies, who pointed his sword and said You will join us. His name had been Aldous, and half a year ago he had been a Kestrel; the locket was still at his throat. He closed through Jetten's entangling vines, hit Morgash with a sword too cold to touch, reached out his fingers — Join me — and drew something out of him. Morgash felt the wound close. Something underneath it did not.

That was the whole of the Hollow's design in a sentence, and the table understood it at once. Every drained hit left permanently less of a person until they got out and slept in a real bed, and there is no real bed in a tomb. Grief, made into a rule. Thomas killed Aldous with First Light and then stood over the body reading Marn's journal in his head — Aldous's wounds closed, but he did not mend. Edda would not leave him — the same words the dungeon had just used for Morgash. He said out loud that he was afraid of having an orc that turned into a wight. He was wrong about the mechanism, as it happens, and nobody ever corrected him, because the fear drove the right decisions.

The talk of undying things drew a thing out of Gren. He showed the others the acorn his elders had pressed into his hand the day they sent him north: from the heart-oak of his warren, still green after all these years, to be planted when he found the place he belonged. I still have not found the place where I belong.

Osric, the Keeper

The doors opened on a long pillared hall, a platform in the dark at the far end, two more wights, a crowd of skeletons, and a wight on a throne who beckoned them forward. Gren asked if they should come in and parley. Come in. Jetten asked what he had done with Edda. She's here. She's with us. She's happier. Thomas asked why they should not simply destroy him here and now, and Osric, who had managed courteous for five hundred years and did not suffer insolence, told him: I thought you would be interesting, but your insolence irritates me. You will not make a suitable wight. You'll make an excellent zombie.

Edda, the caster Marn's journal had promised, froze Jetten where he stood with a word. Morgash closed on her, missed, refused the miss, knocked her flat, and then hit her so hard that the spell broke in her mouth and Jetten was free — the best sequence of the night. Osric worked him over for it, drain after drain, until there was two-thirds of an orc left standing. Then Gren opened the driftglobe the party had carried since the first cave and let it burn as daylight, and the whole room lit, and Osric and Edda shrieked and recoiled from it, and every swing they took for the rest of the fight was made at a disadvantage. A skeletal mage answered cleverly with a sphere of darkness over the globe, and Gren spent his next turn simply carrying the one-pound thing out of the dark by hand. Edda tried to command Morgash and he shook it off, and the maul that had broken her spell broke her too. Thomas held his bane through every drained hit, found a perfect seam in Osric's guard, and when it was time, asked for exactly what he wanted: I don't like this guy. I'm going to chop his head off. The wight's head went up, came down, and rolled down the stairs.

"Not so cocky now, are you, Osric." — "You should have talked to us, Osric." — "I hate Osric."

Hesper, the Mortician

Through the east doors: a wight on a dais between two zombies, who looked up and did not fight. She saw the blood on them and knew where it was from. That means Osric is finally dead, or you would not be standing in that doorway. He was very old and very tired. I hope he was courteous with you.

She told them the origin of everything, in her own words. Three hundred, four hundred, five hundred years ago, she and Osric and Cadoc had been treasure hunters. They found a glowing shard in a cave — warm, beating, the size of two cupped hands — and could not put it down. They brought it here to keep it safe. In time their greed became undeath. They cut the stair so no one else could take it. They cut every way in they knew. And so they stood with the Graveheart, lonely, but it gave them life, and it waited, guarded by Cadoc, for the next body to be prepared as a wight to keep them company. But I'm tired. I don't want to do this anymore.

Cadoc, she said, would not speak to them as she did. He was the one closest to it, the one most inured with it; he would only try to kill them. There was no bargaining with Cadoc. The zombies would not hurt them, and would die with her. Morgash asked whether she could undo what the drain had done, and she could not. She told them to take what was in her chest — a bone key for the locked west doors, and a draught of wholeness, the one cure in the world for what the Hollow took — I have no use for either anymore. I'm sure you will need them both. Gren asked how she would like it done. She walked up to the slab and laid her head on it. When it was over both zombies collapsed where they stood, in the same moment, like cut strings.

What the narrator knows

Hesper had just killed the party's favourite theory without any of them noticing. They had suspected, since the tonic and the guards on the tree, that Greenrest was behind the dead under the hill. Hesper had dated herself at three to five centuries; the village was younger than that; and had anyone said the word "Greenrest" to her, she would have answered, honestly, I don't know that word. Nobody asked. The kill-shot never fired. The three treasure hunters had never learned what the shard was, either: there is no image of the Dreamer anywhere in the shrine, no name, no story. Everything they carved, they invented, and it was worn past recognition before the town above them was founded.

What the narrator knows

Hesper was also the first person in the campaign to say the shard's four beats — warm, beating, the size of two cupped hands — and nobody at the altar an hour later thought to connect three greedy treasure-hunters found one warm beating shard with there are three more out there. The Graveheart's own description said it: one chamber of a heart, and a heart has four. It waited in the description for anyone to read closely, and the four ruins stayed a menu for another two sessions before they became a countdown.

Morgash drank the draught, and all the hit points that had not come back came back, and the darkness went out of him. Gren's memory of temple lore offered the rest of them a cure, mostly right: a real bed, not a dungeon floor, would close the drained maximums. Let's charge in, Gren said, because it's time. The clock is ticking.

Cadoc, and the Graveheart

The west doors, unlocked with Hesper's key, opened on a hooded figure with a scythe wreathed in black fire, a ritual altar, a heap of skeletons with lit eyes — and, mounted in a container on the altar, a large crystalline heart.

Cadoc shot Thomas across the chamber and reached out with the black fire and took sixteen hit points off his ceiling in one touch. Morgash was the engine: closed, refused a miss, knocked the guardian flat, surged, hit twice more. Thomas kept Cadoc's guard sapped and spent his last spell on a smite that burned with an extra die for the undead. Gren, out of his best options, threw bolts of guiding light. The skeletal mage was a menace to the end — darkness again, an ice knife, a volley of missiles that put Morgash on the floor, where his orcish blood stood him back up at one hit point for the second time in two days. The kill came the way the best kills do: Cadoc swung his scythe at Morgash and missed so badly that Morgash's riposte was already coming back, and then the Maul of Momentum came down. The maul crushes his skull.

Jetten took the Graveheart, and the vision fired. A beautiful green woman, glowing, white leaves crowning her head. Crying. As she sobbed she closed her hands over her chest and her heart burst; the pieces scattered into the dark, hardening as they flew; the burst drove the mists back from where she stood in a great ring, baring a green valley beside a beautiful lake. Where she had stood, a tree — grey, lifeless, asleep. A single faint heartbeat, and it was over.

Nobody said the word Greenrest out loud. The grey tree in the vision was the grey tree in the square, and every one of them knew it. They stood in the Hollow's heart, battered, three of the four of them permanently less than they had been that morning, with the first shard of the Broken Heart in their hands and no idea yet that a heart has four chambers.
