Part Four

The Heart Knot

In which a squirrel comes back, a goddess makes her last offer, a god answers in the fog, and a village asks four friends for its life.

Chapter IXThe Heart Knot

The Thornheart's first raising went to Pudgy. Invictus read the stone's promise aloud, still in the dragon's hall — once between dawns, a body dead no more than ten days — and pressed it to the squirrel before anyone could argue for anyone else. Pudgy woke. Did we kill the dragon? Did we beat him? Gren told him that Honeysuckle and Pinenut had not made it, and fed him nuts, and Pudgy asked one thing: Will you promise to bury them outside, please, Master Gren? They slept in the geased druid's camp south of the hall. Jetten did not sleep; he never does.

The Duskheart speaks

In the morning Jetten's eyes went black as ink and he spoke, in a voice that was and was not his, Shar's last offer, verbatim:

Invictus. Killing my high priest was regrettable, but it is forgivable. My offer still stands. Take the heart shards and Midnight to the Dreamer's tree. Find the tree's heart knot and plunge the blade into it. Midnight will drain the power from the tree and the shards. The Dreamer will be trapped. Each shard can grant you a resurrection, and the heart of the tree will grant you a fifth. You will win. You will get your family back. The town will win. The Dreamer's summer will remain in Greenrest forever. I will win. My will shall be done. Think carefully, Invictus. This is my last offer.

Midnight glowed while he read it and stopped after. The Duskheart took control, Jetten said. I don't remember saying those words. I know I said them, but it wasn't me.

Invictus was genuinely torn, and said so. What's one fae versus an entire town? Either way the heart was gone, and his family stayed dead. He admitted the pain was easy to bear now and everything was foggy — I think that's what Shar does for her acolytes. I'm infected by her in some way. Takes the pain away from you. His friends did not let him have it. Jetten: It sounds like a trap. Like a bargain with the devil. It's the evil trying to work its way into you. Don't turn to it. Morgash: It seems very obvious that you will think it's the best thing to do if you are getting everything from this. You lost your bearings when you killed that guy. You lose your vow. And now you're completely lost, my friend. Jetten reminded him that the sword had been First Light once, and Invictus said Oh — I don't even remember that. Morgash: It's not the same sword. Don't lie to yourself. Gren: You haven't done anything wrong. You don't need redemption. But if you do this, you will need redemption.

Invictus heard them. I have to rely on my friends here and say I can't take her up on that offer. It just seems like the evil thing to do. He asked to go outside and pray.

The Dawnfather in the fog

On the lakeshore in the early morning he knelt with the psalter a priest had given him, recited the psalms, held the black sword up to the sky, and begged Lathander to release him from Shar's power. The fog curled away from the water, and the Dawnfather was in it.

"You are not completely lost, my child, but the sword you wield must be left here. Bury it. First Light is gone."

They dug a hole together and buried Midnight in the ground of the Wyrmwood. Invictus had Gren swear Pudgy to secrecy — What is? I don't know anything. Yes, sir. Honeysuckle and Pinenut were buried by a tree with acorns, a place where a squirrel could come and say hello. Pudgy went ahead to tell the forest their deeds. Invictus took a plain scimitar out of the party's stores and kept his shield, and on the way out said: I hear that buried sword calling to me and screaming. I have to get out of here before I lose my mind.

The fourth dream

Late in the morning, crossing Greenrest's limits, Jetten's moonstone lit and all four of them fell into the dream on their feet. The bare hill; the fog close; the tree where she had stood — and the whole town present in their nightclothes, no mist between them: Oswin, Selma, Enid, Ambrose, Warin, Pip. And she looked at them.

"You've been here before — in the summer, at the fog, at the breaking. You are the ones holding my heart. I want you to know I forgive him. I forgive Corin. I know he chose to leave me. But he was right. I forgive him. And now, after centuries, I can finally forgive myself for holding on so long, for all of it. I'm asking you, begging you: free me. Bring the hearts to my tree. Find the heart knot. Place them in it, one by one, and I will absorb them. On the fourth, I shall be free. Please, help me. I've been trapped for centuries."

As she faded she looked at Invictus and smiled a little. They woke at the town's edge. My vote is to go straight there and do it, Invictus said. I'm feeling a lot more moral clarity now. Hey — what was I thinking before?

The mayor in the road

Oswin met them in the road near the square, out of breath, with Pip beside him. I know what you're doing. I saw you in the dream. Please don't do this. We'll do anything. You don't have to let her go. She's been here the whole time — what's the difference? A key to the city. Live here as long as you want. Free meals for life; Ambrose had already agreed. I'm fond of you all, and that's what makes this so hard. The town will never prosper again. The fog, I'm sure, will come back.

Invictus: It's just evil to keep her trapped there. You know that.

It was Pip who decided it. I think he's right, Mayor. They need to do what they need to do. And the mayor of Greenrest, who had never once had to think about the tree in fifteen years, looked at a halfling child and said: Oh, Pip. You're right. Very well. I'll tell the guards to stand down. Do what you need to do. I'll go and tell the elders.

What the narrator knows

Two pleas in one morning, for opposite things, from people the party liked. Hers in the night, his in the road. Both of them were right. Greenrest would die the day the Dreamer did — the winters would get real, the harvests would fail, the mists would return — and every word Oswin said was true, and the party had just dreamed the counter-argument in Corin's own voice. The town did not turn on them because they gathered shards. It turned because it was asked, and refused, and was afraid. The watch stood down. The neighbours did not.

The square

The knot was there in the trunk when they looked for it, a heart-shaped seam in the grey bark, and placing a shard into it took a full turn of concentration — interrupted, start over; absorbed on the placer's next turn. Nobody in the square wanted to kill anybody. Three mobs came anyway: the whole street, the farmhands, the torchbearers. Get them! They can't take our town away! Stop this!

Gren went first, as he generally did, and put a hypnotic pattern over the whole street, and the whole street stood and stared at it for the rest of the fight. Jetten began the Duskheart. Invictus gave his voice thunder and told the farmhands to think again, and they paused; Morgash told the torchbearers the same, with a look, and they paused too. Nobody swung. Gren threw a scorching ray into a wall as a warning that impressed no one. The Duskheart went in. Jetten's vines rose out of the cobbles and pinned the torchbearers where they stood. Invictus put his hands up and refused to attack, and was pelted with cabbages and tomatoes for it, every one of which bounced off his plate. Morgash began the Graveheart. Jetten stood between the mob and the tree and beckoned the air to bang every shutter in the square — nobody was impressed by that either. Invictus called a celestial horse out of nothing and mounted it. The Graveheart went in. A pitchfork came at Morgash and his pike came back on the riposte and put a torchbearer down hard, and the club and the sword that followed found gaps in his armour, and he stood there and let them. Gren began the Tideheart. None shall pass! he announced, and tapped a farmhand's knee with the flat of his staff. The Tideheart went in. Invictus rode to the tree, leaned out of the saddle, and pressed the Thornheart into the knot with the words This is for you — and blot out all my iniquities, Lathander — and a tomato took him square in the eye, and he kept his seat. Jetten's vines failed and he threw caltrops and cast the vines again and caught Gren and himself in them.

The Thornheart went in.

The passing

On the fourth heart the knot opened, and the dryad stepped out of the trunk holding the whole heart. Tall, blond, wood-and-blossom, the woman from every dream, in the waking world for the first and last time. She looked at them.

"It's stone. It has been stone in your hands for so long, warm and hard, and now it beats inside of me again."

She closed her hands around it. The light went out of the facets. The edges lost their shape. It went soft and warm and wet, and by the time she lifted it to her breast it was not a jewel at all. It was a heart — plain, red, ordinary meat, beating in her hands. Because the stone had never been the magic. The stone was the part of her that could not die. She put it back where it belonged. The mob began screaming. The tree went into full bloom — every branch at once, the most alive anything in that valley had ever been — and she was gone.

What the narrator knows

She left without giving them anything, and the table noticed. It was on purpose. The Heart's magic was her; when she went, it went; there was nothing left to hand over but the story. Above Harrow Vane there is always somebody, and the order of Shar knows that a Dark Justiciar walked out of its temple and then walked away from the goddess. Midnight is in the ground on a lakeshore, still calling, and only four people and a squirrel know where.

The neighbours walked away muttering and dejected. From somewhere far off and everywhere: Thank you. Thank you so much. I can never repay you. Morgash said Yes, you can. Wait. Pudgy arrived — Oh, boss, I just got back. What happened? — and Gren gave him an acorn, and Pudgy stayed by Gren from that day.

The mist comes home

The mists rolled slowly back into the valley. Commoners looked at the four of them, sad and dejected, and would not speak. The elders understood, and could never forgive. Oswin said: Please. Please just go.

And Invictus came to, the way a man surfaces from deep water, and said: Thomas. My name is Thomas.

EpilogueThe First Autumn

Leaves fell in the square that autumn for the first time in living memory, and the children of Greenrest had never seen it and did not know what to do, and then did. Oswin watched from the steps of his tower. A few families took the Trade Way south for the winter, and it was a real winter. The harvest was smaller. It was also real. And one evening at a sick child's bedside, Acolyte Enid prayed to the Earthmother as she had prayed ten thousand times before, and for the first time in Greenrest's history something answered. Chauntea had forgiven the town. Greenrest had a healer of its own.

Morgash found that ten thousand gold in gems was the big score after all. Warin Holt asked him to hold the gate at Hedgewatch, and he stayed — I feel responsible — and in time the town forgave him, and Oswin always stood him breakfast and dinner, and he had a permanent room at the Full Larder.

Jetten stayed friends with Thomas and came and went. One evening the moonstone at his throat glowed green for the last time, and he had a real dream of his sister, sent by a fey who owed him, and he said goodbye to her properly. In the morning the stone was milky white.

Thomas went home. A month later in Daggerford he mended things with the temple of Lathander and walked to the five graves in the walled yard, and read every name aloud, and then his own, and stayed until it was dark. The grief ended there — not the memory, the grief. He became a paladin again, properly, and went wandering with Jetten for some years after, the two of them, slaying evil where they found it.

Gren learned that every squirrel in the Misty Forest knew that Gren the Mighty had freed the Green Lady, and his warren, which had sent him away, sent for him, and there was a feast. He came back to Greenrest afterward and planted the Everseed Acorn in the square beside the old tree, and stayed. It grew into a young oak that drops its leaves every autumn, as an oak should. Pudgy became the prophet of a small religion whose members reluctantly tolerate Morgash. Gren's old debt to the orc is paid in acorns, one by the bedside every morning; Morgash steps on them about half the time.

Nobody was wrong enough to make it clean. They freed a soul a thousand years caged, and they turned off a village's good fortune in front of the village, and both of those things are true. Somewhere past the fog, Corin's story ended a long time ago, and nobody will ever know how. But she stopped waiting for it, which is the only ending anyone gets.

Played across nine sessions, 7 July to 29 September 2026 — Thomas A. Invictus, Morgash the Gravemaker, Gren Greenmantle, Jetten Elisedil, and the one behind the screen.

AfterwordWhat the Narrator Knew

A campaign about a grief nobody could see is also, unavoidably, a campaign about what four people chose to look at. These are the narrator's notes gathered in one place — what the company saw, and what stayed just out of view.

What they did well

Credit first, because it is real, and because there is more of it than of anything else. The party found the campaign's opening clues faster than the campaign expected them to. They read Selma's label aloud before they had ever seen the village. They chose, on their first night, to sleep without the tonic — the single most important decision anyone made in nine sessions, and it was made from suspicion alone. Gren named the dryad from a dream on the first morning, and Thomas connected her to the tree in the square inside a minute. Morgash read the displacer prints at the trailhead and said so out loud, and read the bullywugs' glowing eyes before an arrow flew.

They were as good with people as with prints. Morgash gave Oswin the honest version at a breakfast that was going wrong, and mended it. Jetten kept a portrait back from a sale on a hunch, carried it to a shopkeeper's counter, and it produced the name Thomas had crossed a forest for. Morgash held a friend back from a murder with his arms and an argument, and when the friend chose anyway, stayed his friend. On the lakeshore, with a goddess's last offer on the table and every resurrection in the world in the balance, four people talked a fifth back from it in ten minutes of plain speech. They freed her at the cost of everything the Heart could have given them — and spent the first Thornheart dawn on a squirrel, without being asked twice. Of every door in the last session, that one was the smallest, and it is the one that says most about who they were.

Threads for the next story

A good campaign leaves more behind than it uses. These are the ones still lying in the road, for anyone who goes back that way.

The Traveller's Letter — addressed in a careful hand to someone in Daggerford, still sealed in Jetten's pack. Somebody in Waterdeep is waiting for a merchant who will not come home.

The fey temple — a line on the lintel, a light, and a road home, and beneath the shrine a thin place the displacer pack came through. The watchtower above the valley overlooks both roads to it.

Warin's barracks — "I don't want to bother Oswin with such things." The constable's fuller theory of the Kestrels' end is still his to tell.

Coll's empty plot, and Tess by the tannery — a grave dug for people who never came home, and a woman in Daggerford who never got Marn's letter. Both are one visit from closed.

Hesper's word — the one question nobody put to a five-hundred-year-old woman: Greenrest? She would have said she did not know it, and half a campaign's theory would have gone quiet.

The Widow Fen — a drop-stone on the moor path, a jar marked ours in a lower room, and an annotated Volo's Guide nobody opened.

The Iris — a shut stone eye at the centre of a temple of the Lady of Loss, passed twice with a prisoner and a priest more urgent than a carving. Above Harrow Vane there is always somebody.

The Daggerford ledger office — where a scribe once started pulling threads, and where Veck's other customers may still keep books.

The Wyrmwood — rooms north of the hall unexplored, a druid's story untold, and a black sword in the ground on the lakeshore that has not stopped calling.

Corin — a name said aloud in the first two dreams and never followed. What became of him past the fog is the one thing nobody in this story ever learned. A ballad sheet on the roadside remembers him; so, perhaps, does a raven-haired family somewhere.

None of what stayed shut changed the ending. Four hunters came down the Trade Way after a relic and left having given it away; they were asked, twice in one morning, for opposite things by people they liked, and they chose the harder one and lived with the square's silence afterward. A mystery is the sum of what its detectives pick up, and the thing these four picked up, in the end, was a stranger's thousand-year grief, and they carried it home. That is the part worth keeping.
