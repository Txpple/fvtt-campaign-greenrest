Part Three

The Count

In which the tree in the square begins to wake, the dreams begin to tell the truth, and a village that never wept starts to dream again.

Chapter VThe Long Rest

They walked out of the Hollow into a silence that told them the place was spent. Thomas could not carry his own shield; the party carried it, and Morgash found him a stick for a cane. Jetten made a proper map of the way home, so perfect he could have sold it, and they came to the edge of Greenrest at dusk without a single wrong turn. Warin's scout waved them in as heroes. Word of the displacer pack had spread. Ambrose fed them and would not take coin.

A healer was sent for Thomas. She waved holly, prayed to Chauntea, rubbed healing herbs on his grey face — and nothing happened, nothing at all. She suggested the church infirmary. It was the first time the party had seen it plainly: no divine magic answers in Greenrest. They put that down to a bad healer.

What the narrator knows

There has never been a cleric in Greenrest. No child of the valley has ever knelt and received the Weave. The only cure in town is Selma's — pure herbal alchemy, no god's grace in it, unusually potent because the valley's soil grows healing herbs as impossibly as it grows everything else. Every flask she sells is the Dreamer's gift wearing the Earthmother's name. So is every harvest. So is the tonic.

The first dream

That night, in real beds, all four of them dreamed together for the first time — one dream, one place, all four of them standing in it. A summer hillside. A raven-haired stranger laughing. A feeling of being adored that was not theirs. She was there and did not see them; when she drifted near and reached out it was through them, at someone else, and she called that someone Corin. Stay this time, she said to the hillside. Stay, and I'll make it summer for you forever. Jetten's moonstone glowed green inside the dream. In the morning the drained maximums had closed and Thomas could nearly stand without the stick.

Over breakfast they interrogated Ambrose, who answered like a man explaining the weather: the tonic was taken weekly like medicine, since childhood; a dead dryad haunted their dreams; Selma brewed it; it had been that way for generations. Thomas faked a relapse, slipped upstairs, and tested the tonic and the food for poison and disease. Both were clean.

The town day

They spent the recovery days working the town, and the town answered them the way an honest town does.

Selma showed them her wares and explained the recipe — berries, strained apple, a secret garden herb; perfectly harmless, it just stops you dreaming — inherited from the apothecary before her, twenty or thirty years back. The dryad was just "the dreamer." Gren read her carefully and found her honest. They told her the Kestrels' fate, and she opened the silver locket from the camp and found a letter Marn had never sent — four old places… the prize lies in exactly one… the shrine wasn't a shrine. A graveyard — and a house by the tannery in Daggerford where a woman named Tess was waiting for news. Selma promised to have the bodies seen to.

Gully the smith repaired Brakk's claw-rent plate overnight in trade for two of the cold black swords from the Hollow, because he wanted to study them. When the plate came back there was a roll-off for it; Morgash and Thomas both rolled abysmally, and Gren, who could not even wear it, rolled high and declared that neither of them deserved it. Thomas took it.

Mother Wend sold them kit and told them the valley's story as the valley told it: settled around the tree in a fogless valley, endless summer, fruit four times a year, two years of militia service for everyone, and one demographic tell — no elves or gnomes ever stayed; fey-born never seemed to trust the place. One dwarf had, generations back. That was her.

At the church, Thomas walked the nave with the eye of a man who had grown up in one and found that even the original stonework was all fruits and farming — built for Chauntea by people who did not understand her ways, a grand cathedral for a humble harvest goddess. Acolyte Enid admitted, when asked, that the goddess had never answered a single one of her prayers with a spell. She looked around at the gilded luxury and said I didn't choose this, and showed them the basement on prodding, which held nothing worse than gilded farm tools. In the graveyard a contented mourner told them she'd taken the tonic since she was a girl. People in Greenrest, Morgash observed, all said almost the same thing.

What the narrator knows

The church was a clue in stone and the party walked right through it. The relief over the door — a winged, blossoming figure the locals call Chauntea — carries none of the Earthmother's signs: no sheaf, no sun, no rose. It is the Green Lady wearing the Earthmother's name. Grove and fey trappings everywhere, too many, wrong for the goddess. The grandeur is gratitude architecture, raised over generations out of the overflow of a sleeping fey's grief — a building full of worship and empty of divinity. Enid was the purest believer in the wrongest faith, and the silence she had never noticed was a refusal.

Coll complained he had no work, and admitted that he sometimes skipped his tonic, got the dreams, and went back on it. Gren asked him to skip it again and talk next time. He lit up at the job of taking scouts out for the displacer carcasses and the two lockets on the wights' bodies: Finally, something to do. Pip reported, in passing, the session's sharpest clue, delivered by a child: Selma was nervous and unhappy that the tree was growing.

And Oswin, at his tower, gave them the mayor's-line history and a confession: he and Selma both privately believed the valley's bounty was the dryad's, not Chauntea's — that cleric has never cast a real divine spell in my life. Shown the charcoal sketch of the weeping woman from the Kestrels' crate, he knew her at once, from his own childhood dreams. That's her. That's the dryad. Then he walked them out to the square, and there were buds on the tree. The first new growth in the town's living memory. Double heroes, he called them.

What the narrator knows

The town day was the party's best chance to test its theory, and the day the theory tested them instead. Gren read Selma with an honest man's insight and found her honest — and the company, understandably, trusted the roll a little less than they trusted their suspicion. Nobody followed the name Corin, which the dryad had now said aloud to them twice; the dreams would explain it for them soon enough. And Jetten's moonstone had now glowed in two dreams, and he had not asked anyone about it, which is an elf's way. It is the trap suspicion sets for everyone: a party wary of the whole town was, that day, curious about none of it in particular.

Jetten walked past the tree with the Graveheart in his pouch, and it beat harder the closer he came, and softer walking away. They said it out loud to each other afterward. The shard and the tree were one thing.

The second night the dream came again, the same hillside, the same greeting — Corin? Stay this time. And this time Jetten did something nobody had scripted. He held out the Graveheart. Her eyes widened. And the dream cut.

What the narrator knows

In a thousand years of dreaming that was the one moment the dream looked back: not her recognising them — she would not see them for three more shards — but her heart recognising a piece of itself. The party would not understand what they had done until the fourth dream, when she said you've been here, in the summer, at the fog, at the breaking. I thought you were memories I hadn't met yet.

Longshadow

They chose the Widow Fen for the second site, because it was closest and Thomas's worry about the hags had faded, and they bought antitoxins because the dragon was for later. On the road out a man-shaped shadow crossed the ground with nothing above it to cast it, and the squirrels, when Gren asked, gave him the name: Longshadow. Flying stag. Feeds on people. Time to hide.

Longshadow and two lesser perytons came down on them at the old temple ruin. Jetten spent the fight as the flock's favourite meal and lost his concentration twice to their talons; Gren's fire found them twice in the perfect place and the dice mocked him for it; Thomas learned his new plate turned every claw that came at it, and killed the last one on First Light. And Morgash, the moment Longshadow stooped into reach, swung the maul once and killed it in the air — and then, in two turns, gave a complete demonstration of everything a Battle Master is for, and killed the second peryton too.

Gren gave a feather to a squirrel as proof and launched a forest-wide campaign of information. Gren the Mighty has slain Longshadow! Six squirrels came back bearing acorns in tribute. This was not a joke. It would matter.

Chapter VIThe Widow Fen

Thomas called a steed out of the fey and rode a thing that was part plant and part horse north-east from the ruin. The ground softened, the muck-smell rose, and the forest gave up at the edge of open black water. Out in the fog stood a great tree with cabins and rope bridges in its crown, lights burning.

Morgash read the bullywugs before they read him: fey-born half-frogs, normally keeping to their own society, but these had a purplish-red glow behind the eyes that was not natural. Enthralled. Jetten nocked an arrow.

The first fight in the swamp almost ended everything. A bullywug bog sage leapt into range and dropped a sphere of acid over all four of them, and all four of them failed to get clear — Gren burned his luck on a second try and failed again. The sage killed one of its own warriors in the blast and hopped up and down, delighted with itself. Then the acid burned on, and Thomas and Jetten both went down. Jetten came back on his own healing word and put Thomas up; Gren's magic missiles killed the sage and he healed Thomas up off the floor again; Morgash carried the melee alone, knocking bullywugs flat with the maul while the others found their feet. When it was over the sage had done six times the damage of its four warriors put together. Jetten produced the trick of the night — a rope into a pocket of nowhere, and the whole party climbed up into it to rest where nothing could follow.

The treetop village was five short fights and one long revelation. Bullywugs on the bridges. Animated brooms, a flying sword, two rugs that tried to smother — the party took six points of damage across all five combats, and Gren, who had whiffed three fights running, snapped the flying sword in half with a critical burst. The rooms were the weight.

The Widows' Ledger. Thomas knew the cipher on sight; he had copied it a hundred times in Daggerford. A bill-of-lading book for the delivery of body parts, with a drop-stone marked on the moor path they had walked past that afternoon. The entries stopped six months back — right around when his family died. He said it out loud: This is exactly why I'm on this quest.

The Sisters' Notes, read aloud by Jetten, reframed the campaign: The heart is broken in four. RED — the barrow. BLUE — ours, below. GREEN, BLACK — not yet found. The dryad sleeps in the great tree on Greenrest's square. The heart is hers. Whole, it beats four times. One beat brings back our sister. Propose partnership. Do not fight. The hags knew they were coming, and the hags knew everything.

The grief-jars. Mourner's Teeth. Widow's Tears. A Mother's Little Finger. Thomas stared at the last one — how is that not my wife's finger in the jar? — and Gren checked the teeth and told him, flatly and kindly, that they were adult teeth. Nothing of his children's.

The sketches. Dozens of pages of the same hard-jawed, sharp-eyed woman with a greatsword, and on the back: HER. The one who took our sister. Do not let us forget her face. Morgash knew Lae'zel instantly. She had told him once, at a riverside teahouse outside Baldur's Gate, about killing a hag. In an oilcloth wrapped and rewrapped until the folds went soft was a pressed flower and a little carved sign, ALL WELCOME, from that teahouse. Morgash learned that the hags had been drawing his lost love's face for years, and said: That woman was awesome.

The parlour

Down the hollow trunk, past the waterline, was a parlour. Jetten picked the cage's lock with his dagger and the little girl — "Masie" — thanked them, said she had been taken, and started asking questions. How many of you are there? They said you were bringing a stone; do you have the stone? What are your names? Gren read her as wrong. Morgash broke it: the girl walked up and started smelling him, said I smell Lae'zel on you, and he swung, and his hand went straight through her. The illusion dropped and two hags stepped out of the dark.

The offer was made plainly. Not only did she kill our sister — she killed your heart too. Help us gather the Heart; one beat wakes Ethel; the other three are yours. Resurrect the world for all we care. We just need our sister. And for Thomas, the hook with the barb in it: they knew who had supplied them from Daggerford. I don't care about your family. But I know who did it — the same ones who bring us the body parts. Thomas wanted the deal. The price the hags wanted was an unbreakable bond, death for the one who failed; Thomas offered a paladin's oath instead, and Hazel told him what she thought of it: The oath of a vengeance paladin means nothing to me. Jetten refused outright. Gren pushed for something free and got nothing. Hazel called it. These aren't going to bargain with us. Let's kill them, sister, and be done with this.

It was not close. Gren looked Hazel in the eye — I did not forget any of my spells — and dropped a fireball into the room so carefully shaped that it burned every animated thing in it and none of his friends. Morgash caught Mabel with a stroke that found the perfect seam, knocked her down twice, and when she blinked away told her no matter where you go, I'm going to find you. Thomas broke Hazel's hex with a smite. Jetten's arrow killed Mabel. Hazel, bloodied and on her knees, was offered surrender, and spat. You just killed my sister. Do you think I'm going to make any deal with you? I'd rather die. Gren tried to hold her for questioning and she shook it off. Morgash asked if he could take her alive, and she looked up at him and said Do it. I have nothing to live for. He smashed her skull, and a blue stone rolled out of her hand as she fell.

The Tideheart showed whoever lifted it the same vision the Graveheart had: the dryad weeping, her heart bursting, the mists driven back. Two of four.

What the narrator knows

The drop-stone was marked on the moor path the party had walked past that afternoon. Nobody went back to it. The fen had a lower room whose jar was labelled ours and was never found; an annotated copy of Volo's Guide was looted and never opened. And the name Thomas had crossed the Misty Forest for was in that parlour, in two heads, and the party let the parley collapse into a fight inside three exchanges. The hags had asked for a bond; Thomas had offered an oath; Jetten had refused; and Hazel drew the only conclusion she could. It was a good fight, and a fair one to pick. It was also the moment the campaign's one purchasable truth walked out of reach — though Thomas would find the name anyway, at a shop counter, by keeping the right thing and asking the right person.

Thomas went straight to the jar-wall. Sources, prices, dates, years of them, all in the same hand as the ledger upstairs. Every Daggerford shipment accounted for. These two had been the buyers. And nowhere on that wall was the name of the man who sold. In a cage of packed straw in the corner lay a child's skeleton with MASIE scratched on the chest; the real one had died a long time ago. Among the tea-table loot they kept back one thing from sale: a gilt-framed portrait of a merchant nobody recognised. Jetten thought someone in town might.

What the narrator knows

The hags had been the one place in the campaign where the truth could be bought instead of dreamed, and they had died with most of it unsaid. Ethel, their sister, had died near Baldur's Gate at Lae'zel's hand. The partnership they proposed was the first read on who at the table would be tempted to keep the Heart, and Thomas's eyes had gone up. Nobody, hag or hunter, knew what became of Corin past the fog. Nobody ever will.

Chapter VIIThe Temple of Shar

They came home to Greenrest at nightfall with the shops shut and took the inn. Gren claimed a human-sized bed to himself and scattered caltrops in front of the door in case of Pip. And in real beds, with two shards in the house, they dreamed the Summer.

All four of them on the same hillside, high summer, the valley in bloom. A blonde woman under the great tree and a raven-haired man walking up to meet her; the valley bloomed harder every time he smiled. Then a season-montage — never quite winter — with more silver in his hair each visit and none in hers, until the two of them sat under the tree with his silver head on her shoulder, neither smiling, both knowing, and she said it: Stay. I will make it summer for you forever. Be as I am. Never end. Jetten's moonstone glowed as he went into trance. In the morning Jetten's woodcraft put the last piece in: dryads are fey who loved mortals. The dreamer under the tree was the dryad, and this dream was not her grief. It was her offer.

Ambrose met them in the common room to say nobody was eating; everybody was outside. The tree had budded overnight — hundreds of buds and one blossom already open — and a town that had never in living memory seen it bloom stood around it calling it a miracle. The four of them knew it was a count. Gren walked the Tideheart toward the trunk and felt it beat harder, and quieter walking away.

Breakfast with the mayor

Oswin took them to a small restaurant off the square for eggs and asked it plain. For fifteen years I never once had to think about that tree. Now it's all anyone talks about. What is it you keep bringing back to our home?

It went badly first. Gren pushed on the dryad and on Chauntea's silence; Thomas opened the body-parts trade and got a flat denial — not in this town, sir, not without my knowledge; and the mayor listed the free room and board and the eggs nobody had paid for, and stood up to leave, genuinely angry. It baffles me that you don't trust us when we've done nothing but give our open arms to you. Morgash saved it. He gave the mayor the honest, non-secret version: that beyond the town's edge, where Greenrest doesn't go, there had been evil things, and all the party had been doing was clearing them out so the town could prosper, and the tree had started to recover as they did it. Oswin took that, thanked him for finally being honest, and went off to his tower to think. The hearts stayed unmentioned by vote. Thomas got one more thing out of him before he went: the mayor agreed to skip his next dose of the tonic and compare dreams.

Selma did not like any of it. The town had not dreamed in generations; children started the potion at six; this morning was the first time in her life anyone had reported a dream, and her response was to try to make the potion stronger.

Mother Wend was where the session turned. Shown the gilt-framed portrait, she knew the sitter at once — the green waistcoat, the gold signet ring he liked to show off — and checked her ledgers. Renlow Veck. Came through every couple of months, always paid, took the Fen Trail out with sealed boxes and came back without them. Last seen about six months ago, heading home to Daggerford. Gren read the brass plate on the frame aloud — a gift, with gratitude, for my best customers — and Wend, who had assumed the boxes were apples, was appalled. Her dates and the Widows' Ledger matched to the day. Morgash, with a look that would have made a watchman kneel, secured the arrangement: if Veck ever came back, Wend would delay him and send for the constable.

Thomas said it aloud, at the table, for the first time: I know who was responsible for my family's assassination.

The Iris

A day's march north, a dreamless camp, and on the second morning: a ring of pale stone in an old meadow, broad steps up from the south, four hooded statues watching inward, and at the centre a disc of stone cut in curved petals meeting at a point, like a shut eye. No door. Nobody to meet them. Stairs cutting down under the stone on the far side. Gren asked the local squirrel, a round one named Pudgy, who reported people in purple robes who lived down the stairs and mostly came out at night, and left a hero-worshipper with a nut. Morgash found no footprints on the stone, and no cobwebs either.

Harrow Vane came up the stair to meet them, and was not surprised. He opened with Thomas's full name and his dead wife's. Thomas Invictus of Daggerford. Ann's husband. You'll forgive me — we've followed you and your work for some time. The pitch came in pieces, and every piece was true. Thomas's power did not come from Lathander; he was an Oath of Vengeance paladin, it came from the oath, and the oath was built out of what had been taken from him. Shar is the Lady of Loss. Thomas had lost, and turned it into power, and that is rare and valuable. We're not asking you to be a supplicant. We're asking you to be a leader. We want you to be a Justiciar of Shar. Yes, they had a piece of the heart — the Duskheart — and yes, it could be part of the bargain. And they had Renlow Veck. He's for you, Thomas.

What the narrator knows

The Sharrans had had nothing to do with the murders, and said so, and it was true. They had taken Veck within weeks of them, for reasons of their own — which is why the Widows' Ledger stopped: the supplier had been removed. Every cultist in the building had come to Shar the same way, from inside a pain, and been offered oblivion as mercy, and taken it gratefully. And the temple was the campaign's rehearsal for its own ending. It was the one ruin where the party would kill people — mortals with names and reasons, nobody raised or charmed into it, all certain they were right. Whatever the party did here, they would be asked to do again in a village square.

The cell

Veck was bare-chested, wrists bolted to the rock, ragged trousers, the gold signet ring still on his right hand. He started apologising before anyone spoke. Thomas gave his voice thunder and asked why. The confession came out unprompted and complete. It had started with selling names — widows, people with nobody left to come asking. It was only ever money. Then a scribe in the ledger office started pulling threads and would not stop, and Veck went to that scribe's house to frighten him. It went wrong. I know I killed your family, he said, out loud, to the family's husband.

From the doorway, Harrow: That's it, Thomas. Become vengeance. This is what you were meant to do.

Morgash stopped him. Bodily — arms locked around the paladin's chest, feet planted, Jetten helping — and with an argument better than the grapple. You are going to allow this guy that you just met, that knew everything you have been going through and never came to help you a single time — now he wants something from you. He is no friend. He deserves to die, but not here, not now. I told you when I met you that I will be with you, and I will help you get justice. Jetten worked the other side: there was more to this than met the eye; Harrow Vane wanted something in return; and this would not bring the family back. Thomas's rage carried him through the hold, and the ogre-strength in his gauntlets broke Morgash's grip.

Out of honour, I have to kill this guy. End of story.

He drove First Light into Veck's chest, and Morgash said, twice, you will regret this. The sword did not flare. The warmth went out of it. The vision of the Lathander paladin he had seen the night he first held it was simply gone — as if the god himself slunk away from him. The rose-gold ran to ink; the blade drank the candlelight and came back black, crackling with a purple light, and it announced its new name: Midnight.

Then Shar took her payment. He reached for his own first name in memory and there was nothing there, and it did not trouble him, because why would it. He was Justiciar Invictus. Asked about it later that night — I don't know what you're talking about. My name's always been Justiciar.

Harrow pulled down his robe and showed the Duskheart on a chain against his chest, and named the actual price. Never join it. Never wake the sleeping archfey. The heart must never become whole. Four pieces joined were four true resurrections — the wife, the children — and Shar's magic could stretch it further. All the goddess wanted in exchange was that the archfey under Greenrest, a demigoddess and an old friend of Chauntea and Selûne, stay in loss forever. As long as the daughter of her allies suffers, she is happy.

Invictus refused. This man deserved to die, but we still have a job to do, and I can't agree to that. Harrow said that in that case he could not give him the heart, and Invictus put him on the floor with a shield bash and hit him twice with the black sword, and Harrow, unimpressed — did you really think this would be that easy? — spoke a word and was gone. I'll see you downstairs.

What the narrator knows

Shar knew exactly whose heart was in pieces. Keeping her rivals' friend tortured in grief for eternity was the whole point; every beat of the Heart spent, and every beat stretched, was torment kept flowing. The fifth resurrection was not a kindness. It was a cruelty aimed at Selûne through Selûne's devotee's oldest friend. And the name was not erased from the world — only from him. Everyone else still knew it. Jetten still remembered a boy on a ranch.

What the narrator knows

Nobody ever opened the Iris. The shut stone eye at the centre of the ring, and the four hooded faces watching it, were passed twice — on the way in and on the way out — with a prisoner and a priest more urgent than a carving. The Daggerford end of Veck's trade — the ledger office, whoever else had known — was left for another day. And none of the three who watched Shar take a man's name asked him afterward what it had been; they let him be Justiciar for a while, which may have been the gentler thing.

Downstairs

Before following, Morgash cut the ring finger off Veck's corpse, signet and all, to carry back to Greenrest as proof. Upstairs, three cultists stood up and did not know what to do, and Invictus, holding a black sword, told them to kneel and kiss his hand, and one of them did. My Dark Justiciar. I am here to serve. They would not turn on the high priest, but they let the party walk past.

The temple floor was zealots, enforcers and acolytes, and it got ugly in the middle — a zealot's halberd put Jetten on the floor and Invictus's hands brought him back; an acolyte held Morgash rigid with a word; Gren's second careful fireball killed an acolyte and an enforcer together. The two fireballs did most of the fight. Then Harrow Vane, in the summoning circle on the lower level, was a real high priest: black bursts of necrotic power aimed at Morgash two at a time, an unholy word that stunned Jetten and broke Invictus's bane, a column of flame that put Morgash on the floor and could not keep him there. The party answered with everything they had, and Jetten finished it: Goldthorn took the high priest of Shar at the waist, and his chest fell away from his legs.

Harrow dropped a robe that turned blades, a staff that sharpened spells, and the Duskheart — near-black purple, the light crawling under its surface — which Jetten took and attuned. Its gift was very like Shar: advantage against anything that touched the mind, and, once at dusk, the power to reach into a memory and change it. They stripped the temple of gold and velvet and star-charts with no sun on them, and Invictus bluffed them out past the guards on the entrance floor before anyone worked out what had happened below, and made the road home to Greenrest, where a scout waved them in at the town line. Three of four.

Chapter VIIIThe Wyrmwood

The third dream broke the whole town open. With three shards in the house, back on the hillside in the mist, the party were not alone this time: other hazy figures stood in the fog around them, watching too. The dryad and the raven-haired man were arguing, and she was losing.

"Stay — stay, and I'll make it summer for you forever. Be as I am. Never end."

"If you take the ending out of me, there's nothing left that you love. A life isn't for keeping, my dear. It's for spending. Let me go and spend mine."

He turned his back and went down the hill. She followed him exactly as far as a line she could not cross and watched the fog take him. Then time ran fast — real autumns, light winters, brief snow on the orchard — and every evening she waited at the fog-line for a man who never came back. When she finally gave up she tore out her heart and ripped it in four and threw the pieces to the four directions, and the shock blew the mist off the valley in a ring, all at once, and where she had stood she folded down into a tree.

Invictus opened the morning with a confession. I'm feeling like this town is deriving some major benefit from this dryad being pulled up in that tree, and I'm afraid that if we set her free it'll destroy this town. I just don't seem to have my same moral compass anymore. Maybe she deserves to be there. Jetten: It's not right to let her remain enslaved, Invictus. Gren pointed out that it was self-inflicted, and that the man had said life is for spending. Invictus let it drop, for now.

The town that woke up

Oswin met them downstairs, exhausted and electrified. I saw you. You were on the hill. All four of them, and half the town had seen them too. The waitress had dreamed it and could not sleep — it's almost like the explosion made its way into my mind. Ambrose set down three glasses of orange juice with his hands shaking and walked back to the bar without a word. Invictus gave the mayor the careful version — a vision of something long ago, and the party's suspicion that it had to do with the town's endless summer — and Oswin got there on his own: She was offering him eternal summer, eternal life, and he refused. It seems we might be the beneficiaries of that. He asked whether they had seen those gems before, and was deflected. He would call the elders together that day. He was told, for the first time, about a temple of Shar in the forest whose acolytes were vehemently against freeing the dryad. And Morgash put the finger on the breakfast table. Sir, there's children here. I appreciate what you've done, but that's pretty disgusting.

Pip was outside drawing four wedges in the dirt in berry juice: red, blue, purple, green. Those are the gems that I dreamed about. Everybody sees them. I haven't had dreams in so long. Across the field the children were playing The Breaking.

Selma was not good. Her potions were not working; she was trying a double dose; and she had dreamed too — I saw the mist. I saw it roll out like a tide. The whole town is dreaming again. They bought poison resistance and fire breath from her for the dragon, and Morgash offered her a piece of jewellery, still attached, so she could rest assured the body-part trader was not coming back. She dropped it, and thanked him, and gave him a potion of superior healing on the house. Gren muttered about aiding and abetting on the way out.

The squirrels

South-west along Sera's map to a forest lake, and a temple sunk half into it. Three squirrels came out to meet Gren: Pudgy from the Iris, with one of Longshadow's feathers fixed behind his head, and his friends Pinenut and Honeysuckle. Gren demanded proper obeisance and all three bowed. Their intelligence was sound — the big green one sleeps at the far end on the shiny warm thing; the water's bad, don't drink it — and they volunteered to come in and guard him.

Honeysuckle found a secret door in a fountain face; Pudgy squeezed through a collapsed tunnel and came back forty minutes later to report a hallway on the far side — you are a mighty Gren, how did you know? A shambling mound came straight at them and crumbled at Jetten's feet, a mound of shambling vegetables. A horned druid with unearthly green eyes — must serve the master — and a crocodile too big for its doorway came next; the crocodile got Invictus in its jaws twice and died on the black armour of necrotic fire he had wrapped himself in. Pudgy scratched open the last locked door. In a room of pulleys, one lever was rusted solid and two were freshly oiled, and a pull made a promising sound; Pinenut reported the bar across the dragon's doors was gone. Potions down. Master Gren, be careful. That's where we saw the dragon last time. We'll be right behind you, sir.

Bramblemaw

Both doors swung open on a long causeway across the water, crocodiles drifting uninterested below, and the dragon resting on the dais at the far end. It spoke in Draconic. Come for my treasure, eh? You'll die instead.

Bramblemaw moved first, came up the causeway, and breathed. Pinenut, Honeysuckle and Pudgy, who had been standing guard in front of their warlord, all died in the first breath. Jetten took the whole of it and lost his mark; Invictus took most of it; Gren, with the Tideheart's help, held. Then the dragon flew back to its perch and the fight was on. Invictus walked into the water, the crocodiles noticed, and he stepped through the mist and out again directly in front of the dragon and put it on its back with his shield; a psychic spike from the dragon's mind dropped Jetten, and Gren's word put him back up. Morgash, with Sera's bow, missed and refused the miss. The dragon flew and Invictus's shield bash floored it again, and it burned the first of its legendary resistances to stay up. Gren cast a careful slow over it and it burned the second. Morgash's maul knocked it down a third time and he hit it four times while it lay there, and it looked bloodied. The dragon's mind-spike put Invictus down, failing a death save on the floor. Gren put him back up. A crocodile-sized counterspell failed against the dragon's next one.

Round four, the breath came back, and Jetten and Invictus both went down in it. Morgash poured Selma's potion into Invictus and put him on his feet, then knocked the dragon down a fourth time with the maul sizzling with light. Invictus spent two points of his healing touch to stand Jetten up. The dragon's last pounce found Gren and left him at eight hit points. And Gren, with the last big spell he had, shaped a fireball with every ally excluded and said, as he threw it, This is for Honeysuckle, Pinenut and Pudgy, you son of a —. Bramblemaw made its save and took half. It was enough. The Fireball slew Bramblemaw, and the crocodiles, freed of its hold, swam off into the depths.

What the narrator knows

Bramblemaw had never known what the stone was. It had wept over the jewel it hoarded and could not explain why, and could not stop, and was humiliated by strangers seeing it. There was no price that would have bought the shard — not a richer hoard, not a name, not its own life. If even a green dragon, a thing with no softness anywhere in it, broke under the shard, then what was the shard? The party never got to ask. The Thornheart was named for this wood, which grew briar around it the way a body grows scar tissue.

What the narrator knows

The lair was not finished when they left it. Rooms to the north went unexplored, and the druid who had served the dragon fell to Jetten's arrows with his story untold — what a geas had made him do in the Wyrmwood, and for how long, was never settled either way. Three squirrels lay on the causeway with a feather among them.

Behind a door to the north, a glowing green heart. Invictus took it. It was the exact green of the dragon that had hoarded it, which was either a coincidence or was not; it was warm, and it beat, and it showed him the woman and the burst. It could, once between dawns, press itself to a body dead no more than ten days and give the life back. Gren said immediately: Let's go raise Pudgy. Invictus asked which heart would bring back his family, and was told: the whole one. Four true resurrections, when it is combined — which they could do now, if they only knew how. Gren's arcane sense answered: put them together at the source. At the tree.

The hoard was a decanter of endless water, a staff of withering, a coin with Tiamat's face on it, and ten thousand gold in gems. Invictus said the choice was already made, because the sword had been whispering to him. All four pieces of the Broken Heart were in the party's hands. What to do with them was the only question left.
