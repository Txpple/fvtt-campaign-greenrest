The Broken Heart of Greenrest — Session 3

No Crying in Greenrest

August 11, 2026

Down the hill and into the warmest welcome money can't buy: a mayor who greets strangers like family, an inn that won't take a copper, and a town where nobody cries. Then a child's warning about black cats with too many legs, a night of borrowed grief nobody was supposed to dream, and a hunt that ended with a pack of displacer beasts dead in the ruins where the last adventuring company fell.

The Warmest Welcome

At the trailhead above town, Morgash found tracks circling the path where it leaves the forest — an animal, or animals, with a gait no one could count legs for — and every print turned back into the trees. Whatever walks that circle does not enter Greenrest. Forewarned, the party went down anyway, into orchards and full fields and chimney smoke, and were met in the square by a crowd and a beaming Mayor Oswin Applewhite, who welcomed them like family coming home. The tour was long and generous: the Long Rest and its barman Ambrose, the smithy, the church of the Earth Mother, the brew house, Selma's apothecary, the docks, the Full Larder, the garrison of Hedgewatch — and the mayor's own tower on the hill, because the mayor always lives above the town; it's tradition. Rooms, meals, and ale: free. "We love our guests. You don't have to worry about a penny here." Asked about the Broken Heart, Oswin allowed that another company — the Kestrel Company — came asking the same questions some months back, got directions and full supplies, and never came back. Asked whether anyone in town weeps over anything, the mayor smiled: "There is no crying here."

Pip and the Black Cats

The best intelligence in Greenrest turned out to be a halfling child on the inn porch. Pip — taller than Gren, as he made sure Gren knew — admired Gren's Minor Illusion of a green dragon, declined a gold piece because he already has everything he needs, and then asked the question the adults don't: "Did the black cats follow you too?" Big black cats, too many legs, circling the town when the Kestrel Company was here — and again, an hour before this party walked out of the trees. He never told anyone, because he didn't want to bother them. He walked the party to the spot where he'd seen them watching: the very trail they came in on. Jetten put a name to it none of them liked — displacer beasts, fey predators that stalk their prey — and the party started planning around being hunted.

Provisions and Suspicions

Mother Wend at the Full Larder knew all four of their names before they crossed her threshold — this town is small, and it loves to talk. The party emptied most of its shared gold into her shelves: Gauntlets of Ogre Power for Thomas, Bracers of Archery to steady Jetten's bow arm, and a Wand of Magic Missiles for Gren, along with javelins, arrows, caltrops, a lantern, and a pike and greataxe for Morgash's growing arsenal. Thomas passed his lucky stone to Gren, who bonded with it on the spot. At the garrison, Constable Warrenholt gave them the rest of it straight: his scouts say the beasts stalked the Kestrel Company the same way, waited until the company limped home worn out from a ruin, and killed them at an old temple southeast of town — and such creatures never give up a hunt while their quarry lives. His advice: pick the moment yourselves, go at full strength, and kill them first. His men hold the town and nothing beyond it. And at the inn, Thomas found the gravedigger Coll, who said quietly that something hasn't felt right since the Kestrels came through — that they complained of bad dreams, the way he had bad dreams as a boy, before Selma "helped him." That was the last domino: the whole town, it seems, drinks the dreamless tonic every seventh day, from childhood on. And Thomas already carries proof. His own Dose of Greenrest Tonic — the stoppered clay vial found in the hidden temple, among the web-wrapped effects of a fey wanderer who never left it — bears a label in a tidy, careful hand, and he read it to the party again:

"One swallow, seventh-day, as ever. — Selma."

The same seventh-day cadence Coll had just described. Gren made the call for everyone: "Let's spend the night without drinking the tonic. Something's fishy about this town."

The Night Without the Tonic

The night was perfect — and all four of them dreamed the same visitor: a beautiful, pale woman standing on a hill, her hair the leaves and branches of a white tree, weeping. Her grief was not a picture they watched; it poured into each sleeper and found the grief already living there.

  • Thomas stood on the hill with her and felt it pull him back to the murder of his wife and children — how lonely he is, how they are gone from the mortal world forever and can never be gotten back. He took an Oath of Vengeance over that wound; in the dream there was no vengeance in him at all. Only grief.
  • Morgash dreamed first of Baldur's Gate: the day he pledged his love to Lae'zel, the fiery githyanki warrior, and the day she answered — "I cannot love someone in this world. I must go fight and destroy my queen… Goodbye, my Morgash. I will remember you" — and leapt onto a red dragon and vanished into the dark sky. Then the sky went hazy, and he stood on the hill before the tree-woman, feeling nothing but the loneliness of being left.
  • Gren saw her white and glowing and sad, and her sadness became the day the elders of his Misty Forest village asked him to leave. They called it "going to explore the world" — but in the dream he finally asked the question underneath: did they send him away because of the spellfire inside him? Because he was different? Cast out, unwanted, unloved — and still loving his village with all his heart.
  • Jetten should not dream in trance at all — but he did, eyes open, his moonstone necklace glowing bright green the whole while. He sat with the loss of his sister, dead so long ago, and the particular cruelty of an elf's long life: that he must go on for centuries, and hers was cut short, and no stretch of his years will ever hand hers back.

Four sleepers, four separate griefs — one weeping woman in all four dreams. Morning came, and three of them laid it out plainly. Morgash admitted the woman and kept the rest: "I won't give details about my broken heart."

The Dryad and the Gray Tree

Over breakfast, Gren's temple schooling surfaced with perfect, word-for-word clarity, and Thomas's studies backed him: the woman in the dream is a dryad — a fey being who lives an immortal life passing between the shape of an elf and the shape of a tree. Thomas made the connection out loud: the one gray, dying thing in this flourishing town is a tree. In the square they found it under guard — four soldiers, on standing orders from the mayor and the constable, to let no one harm it and let no one so much as chip the bark. The guards say it has always been this way: the one thing in Greenrest that doesn't flourish, watched like a treasure. Jetten's wood-lore declared it simply a dead tree — twice, with total confidence — while Thomas saw something sick rather than gone. Morgash said what everyone was circling: four guards on a dead tree doesn't add up. "Life is not perfect," he told the party flatly, in the middle of the perfect town. Questions for Selma would have to wait, though — the party had resolved to be no one's easy prey.

The Hunt at the Kestrel Camp

They took the constable's advice and the constable's trail, southeast to a ruined temple where the road simply ends: a cold firepit, scattered bones, a chest, a brush pile — the Kestrel Company's last camp. Jetten's eyes found the watcher in the fog long before it wanted to be found: a great cat-thing with tentacled shoulders, sitting at the edge of sight, waiting. He opened like a Gloom Stalker — Dread Ambusher for an extra arrow, Hunter's Mark on the beast. The shot flew perfect. It missed anyway: Displacement kept the beast's true body a step from where it seemed to stand. Then it threw back its head and howled — and the ambush turned inside out. Two more beasts broke from the bramble. Behind them came the alpha, half again their size, unhurried. The party was encircled.

The first rounds were a beating. Blades and arrows swung wide of the displacement's after-images. Gren's Shatter caught two beasts square in the blast — Avoidance, and neither was so much as scratched. A rend put Jetten in the dirt; a tentacle beat him unconscious. Gren answered with Cure Wounds through Subtle Spell — not a word, not a gesture — and Jetten stood back up while Spellfire Burst wrapped Morgash in bolstering flames. Morgash needed them. He had closed on the alpha with Adrenaline Rush and been raked to nothing — and Relentless Endurance refused the fall. He stood at the brink, drank potion after potion, caught his Second Wind, and killed the first beast with a Precision Attack backhand. Jetten tried the textbook counter — Faerie Fire, to paint the beasts in light no illusion could hide — and the beast in the cube twisted out of the save untouched. Then a tentacle crit found Thomas: one good hit from the dark, his Bless broken. The paladin reached for scholarship instead of steel — the Lantern of Revealing. Unhooded, its light did what the spell couldn't: it tore the displacement off the pack. The beasts shrieked — then learned, and played the edge of the glow. Thomas answered with Bane on one beast — every strike it threw afterward fell short — and a Vow of Enmity sworn against another.

Then the tide turned all at once. Thomas landed First Light and rode it with a Thunderous Smite. Thunder cracked across the ruin. The beast flew backward — straight through Morgash's reach — and the orc's opportunity strike killed it in the air. Jetten worked Goldthorn and his +1 dagger in a Nick-mastery weave, Hunter's Mark riding every hit; one dagger-stroke landed so perfect the fey scimitar beside it seemed jealous, and every freed bonus action became a Healing Word. Gren stopped arguing with the displacement and leaned on Magic Missile — wand, then spell slots, volley after volley. He flicked up Shield to slap the alpha's claws off his face. He re-laid Bless over the line with his Fey-Touched casting. His last barrage was an upcast five-dart burst, and it left the alpha staggering. Thomas met it: First Light through the chest, Lathander's light bursting from the wound in one great flare. The beast fell dark. The last of the pack fled leaderless into the forest and did not return. The hunters that could not be called off lay dead in the camp where they'd killed the Kestrels.

What the Kestrels Left

The camp gave up the Kestrel Company's story in objects: a crate stamped by Greenrest's own Full Larder — jars of preserves, a silver kestrel locket, a charcoal sketch of a weeping woman, a strange fey mushroom cap and a petal that never wilts, a purse of gold, and Marn's journal beside Sera's map of the four ruins — and a brush pile hiding the company's arms: Sera's bow and blades and leathers, Marn's crossbow, Brakk's plate armor rent through by great claws, a quiver of enchanted arrows, a mighty maul that hums with stored momentum, and a pearl that returns spent magic to its keeper. All of it went into the party's packs, the journal and the map deliberately saved for reading in safety. Thomas, staring at the larder-stamped crate: "I'm very suspicious of that town. They've given away all this stuff for free." Weary and patched-up, the party walked back down into Greenrest for a safe night's rest — this time with nothing hunting them on the road home.
